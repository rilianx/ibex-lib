Introduction

We consider the nonconvex continuous optimization problem
min_(x ∈ X) f(x)  s.t.  g(x) ≤ 0,  h(x) = 0,      (1)
where X ⊂ ℝ^(n) is a box and f, g and h are twice differentiable functions given by
explicit expressions. Rigorous global optimization solvers return an interval that
provably contains the global minimum value, even in the presence of rounding errors.
Interval branch-and-bound codes such as GlobSol (Kearfott 1996), IBBA (Ninin, Messine, and
Hansen 2015) and IbexOpt (Trombettoni et al. 2011; Araya et al. 2014) reach this guarantee
by evaluating every quantity that affects pruning with outward-rounded interval arithmetic
(Hansen and Walster 2004; Neumaier 2004). The equalities are relaxed to |h(x)| ≤ ε_(h),
and the solver certifies feasible points inside the relaxed region.

In such a solver, each node of the search tree is a box [x] ⊆ X on which the solver
(i) contracts the box with constraint propagation and with linear relaxations,
(ii) computes a lower bound of f over the feasible part of the box, typically as the
optimal value of a linear relaxation made safe with the technique of Neumaier and
Shcherbina (2004) and Jansson (2004b), and (iii) searches for a feasible point that may
improve the incumbent value (the loup). In IbexOpt, step (iii) combines cheap heuristics
(probing and the inner regions of Araya et al. (2014)) with calls to a local NLP solver,
the interior-point solver Ipopt (Wächter and Biegler 2006), whose approximate local
optimum is then certified as feasible by a small box built around it. Calling a local
solver for upper bounding is standard practice in global optimization; nothing in what
follows is specific to Ipopt beyond the fact that it returns Lagrange multipliers.

Two observations motivate this work. First, on a large benchmark the incumbent is usually
optimal, or very close to it, long before the search ends. Most of the remaining effort
goes into closing the gap from below, and the linear relaxations of the problem over small
boxes are the bottleneck. Second, each successful call to the local solver returns not
only a point x̂ but a full primal–dual pair (x̂,μ̂,λ̂), with multipliers μ̂ ≥ 0 for the
inequalities and λ̂ for the equalities. Interval solvers discard the multipliers. Yet weak
Lagrangian duality holds for any multipliers, not only optimal ones: for every feasible x
of the relaxed problem,
f(x) ≥ L(x) − ε_(h)∑_(j)|λ̂_(j)|,   L(x) := f(x) + μ̂^(⊤)g(x) + λ̂^(⊤)h(x).      (2)
A lower bound on f over the feasible part of [x] therefore follows from any lower bound of
the Lagrangian L over the whole box, with no constraints left. When L is convex on [x],
its tangent plane at x̂ is such a bound, and it is tight when x̂ is a KKT point of the
problem restricted to [x]. This is the theorem of Jansson (2004a) and Messine and
Trombettoni (2019) for convex programs.

Our point is that the convexity hypothesis is needed only for L with the fixed multipliers
(μ̂,λ̂) and only on the current box. It can therefore be checked numerically, node by node,
in the original nonconvex problem. Let [H] be an interval enclosure of ∇²L over [x], and
let d ≥ 0 be a vector such that H + diag (d) is positive semidefinite for every H ∈ [H].
Following the scaled Gerschgorin rule of Adjiman et al. (1998), we take
d_(i) = max {0, ∑_(j ≠ i)|[H]_(ij)| − h_(ii)^(L)}, where h_(ii)^(L) is the lower end of
[H]_(ii). Then, for every x ∈ [x],
$$L(x) \ge L(\hat x) + \nabla L(\hat x)^\top (x - \hat x)
          - \frac{1}{2} \sum_i d_i (x_i - \hat x_i)^2 .
\qquad\qquad (3)$$
The right-hand side is separable, so it is bounded below over [x] in closed form, in O(n)
operations once the gradient is known. Combined with (2) and evaluated in interval
arithmetic, it yields a rigorous lower bound on (1) over [x], which raises the node’s
lower bound or prunes the node. When the Hessian is certified positive semidefinite on the
box (d = 0), the bound reduces to that of Jansson (2004a). It then converges to the local
optimum value as the box shrinks around a nondegenerate KKT point.

Contributions.

1.  A certified Lagrangian lower bound for the original nonconvex problem (main
    contribution). It reuses the multipliers of the local solver that already performs
    upper bounding, so it needs no relaxation, no subgradient iterations and no auxiliary
    optimization problem. Its only extra cost is one interval evaluation of the Hessian of
    the Lagrangian per call. It handles nonlinear equalities, through the ε_(h)-correction
    in (2), and boxes on which the Lagrangian is not convex, through the diagonal shift
    in (3); on boxes where convexity is certified it is the bound of Jansson (2004a).

2.  Making the bound cheap: a backoff that applies it only where it pays off, and
    positive-semidefiniteness tests of increasing strength (interval Gerschgorin,
    comparison matrix, midpoint–radius test with an interval Cholesky factorization). The
    bound costs less than 1% of the running time and the time per node grows by at most
    5%.

3.  An experimental study on 298 instances of the IbexOpt benchmark: an ablation (IbexOpt
    without local solver, with the local solver for upper bounding only, and with the
    Lagrangian bound) and a comparison with SCIP 10. With the Lagrangian bound, the
    rigorous solver closes 236 instances, against 228 without it and 220 for SCIP, and its
    solving time drops to 0.55 of the time without it on the instances that take more than
    a second. [TODO: SCIP with  ± 10¹² bounds for both solvers.]

Related work

Rigorous global optimization and upper bounding with local solvers

Spatial branch-and-bound solvers for nonconvex problems include BARON (Ryoo and Sahinidis
1996; Tawarmalani and Sahinidis 2005), Couenne (Belotti et al. 2009), ANTIGONE (Misener
and Floudas 2014) and SCIP (Vigerske and Gleixner 2018). They build convex (mostly
polyhedral) relaxations from a factorable reformulation and call a local NLP solver to
find feasible points. Their bounds are computed in floating point, with tolerances, and
are not guaranteed against rounding errors.

Interval solvers trade speed for rigor (Kearfott 1996; Hansen and Walster 2004; Neumaier
2004). In IbexOpt (Chabert and Jaulin 2009; Trombettoni et al. 2011; Araya et al. 2014),
the lower bound comes from interval evaluation and from linear relaxations, either XTaylor
linearizations or affine arithmetic (Ninin, Messine, and Hansen 2015; Araya et al. 2024).
These relaxations are made safe a posteriori by the dual-based correction of Neumaier and
Shcherbina (2004) (see also Jansson 2004b; Guilbeau et al., n.d.).

Feasible points are certified either by inner regions, i.e. polytopes or boxes proved
entirely feasible (Araya et al. 2014), or by an existence proof in a small box around an
approximate local optimum (Kearfott 2014). Ipopt (Wächter and Biegler 2006) is the local
solver used by IbexOpt for the latter; we keep this upper-bounding scheme unchanged and
reuse its by-products.

How often a global solver calls its local solver is a design choice of every code: BARON
runs local searches in preprocessing and during the tree under its own rules, Couenne
solves on average a fixed number of local NLPs per tree level, and SCIP’s subNLP heuristic
decides at each node from the nodes processed since its last run and its past success
(Vigerske and Gleixner 2018); multistart is also common (Ugray et al. 2007). In all of
them, the local solver serves the upper bound only. Inside rigorous solvers, local search
has also been used for node selection (Neveu, Trombettoni, and Araya 2016; Reyes and Araya
2024), and Charibde (Vanaret et al. 2020) shares the incumbent between an evolutionary
algorithm and an interval branch and bound.

Rigorous bounds from approximate dual information

Weak duality turns any dual-feasible vector into a valid bound. Making this rigorous with
approximate multipliers is a well-established idea for linear programs.

Neumaier and Shcherbina (2004) and, independently, Jansson (2004b) showed how to compute a
safe lower bound on the optimal value of a linear program from an approximate dual
solution, by bounding the dual infeasibility over the finite simple bounds with directed
rounding. Guilbeau et al. (n.d.) review these bounds and correct typographical errors in
the original formulas. This correction is what makes linear relaxations safe inside
interval solvers, IbexOpt included.

Jansson (2004a) extended the approach to convex programs min f(x) s.t. G(x) ≤ 0, Hx = h
with simple bounds, where f and every component of G are convex and the equalities are
linear. Each function is replaced by its tangent at an approximate solution x̃. The dual of
the resulting linear program, evaluated at the approximate multipliers, gives the bound,
which is exact at a KKT point. The method is a post-processing of any nonlinear solver’s
output, and it handles uncertain data and infinite bounds. Messine and Trombettoni (2019)
derived the same bound from a second-order Taylor expansion of the Lagrangian and the KKT
conditions, in the form f(x^(*)) ≥ L(x̃) + (z−x̃)^(⊤)∇L(x̃), with z_(i) the end of [x_(i)]
selected by the sign of ∂_(i)L(x̃). They proposed it to make convex relaxations solved by a
local solver reliable inside interval codes such as Ibex and IBBA.

QIBEX (Elloumi et al. 2025) put this bound to work. It solves mixed-integer quadratically
constrained programs in IbexOpt with a convex quadratic relaxation obtained once from a
semidefinite program. It certifies both the convexity of the relaxation and, via the same
theorem, its lower bound.

All of these works apply the bound to a problem that is convex by construction: a linear
program, a convex program, or a convex relaxation. Our bound applies it to the original
nonconvex problem. In the proofs of Jansson (2004a) and Messine and Trombettoni (2019),
convexity of f and G and linearity of the equalities serve only one purpose: making the
Lagrangian with the given multipliers convex on the domain. We certify exactly this weaker
condition, box by box, from an interval Hessian. When it fails, we restore validity with a
diagonal shift. The primal–dual pair comes from the local solver already used for upper
bounding, so no relaxation is built or solved.

Convex underestimators and the αBB shift

The αBB method (Androulakis, Maranas, and Floudas 1995; Adjiman et al. 1998; Adjiman,
Androulakis, and Floudas 1998) underestimates each general nonconvex term f on a box by
f(x) + ∑_(i)α_(i)(x_(i)^(L)−x_(i))(x_(i)^(U)−x_(i)). This underestimator is convex if and
only if ∇²f + 2diag (α) is positive semidefinite on the box (Adjiman et al. 1998,
Thm. 2.1).

Adjiman et al. (1998) give several rigorous ways to obtain α from an interval Hessian:

-   O(n²) rules: Gerschgorin and scaled Gerschgorin;

-   O(n³) rules: E-matrix, Mori–Kokame, the lower bounding Hessian and the Hertz method;

-   Kharitonov’s theorem applied to the interval characteristic polynomial;

-   an SDP-based rule.

In the scaled Gerschgorin rule (their Thm. 3.13), for any d > 0, taking
$\alpha_i = \max\{0, -\frac{1}{2}(a^L_{ii} - \sum_{j\ne i} \lvert a \rvert_{ij} d_j/d_i)\}$
makes A + 2diag (α) positive semidefinite for every A ∈ [A]. With d = 1 this is our shift;
they also propose d = x^(U) − x^(L).

In their experiments (Adjiman, Androulakis, and Floudas 1998):

-   computing α takes about 2.5% of the CPU time for most rules;

-   the scaled Gerschgorin rule with d = x^(U) − x^(L), the E-matrix rule and the Hertz
    rule are usually the best, but the differences are modest;

-   branching on the variables that most degrade the underestimator speeds up convergence.

Our use of the shift differs in two ways. First, we shift the aggregated Lagrangian with
fixed multipliers rather than each nonconvex term, so cancellations between objective and
constraints, which can make L convex on a box where f and g are not, come for free.
Second, αBB solves a convex nonlinear program per node with the underestimators. We use
the shifted Lagrangian only through its tangent at the local solution (3), a separable
expression bounded in closed form. The bound is weaker than the optimal value of the αBB
relaxation, but it costs one interval Hessian evaluation instead of a nonlinear solve.

Lagrangian underestimates in branch and bound

Van Voorhis (2002) is the closest predecessor: it uses the Lagrangian, not each function,
as the object to underestimate. He solves quadratically constrained programs by a branch
and bound in which the lower bound minimizes a convex underestimator of the Lagrangian
over the box. The underestimator is built term by term for quadratic functions; the
multipliers are updated by subgradient steps in each node; and interval Newton on the
Fritz John conditions discards boxes. That work does not address rounding errors.

He observes that obtaining good multipliers takes several iterations, and suggests using
Lagrangian underestimates “at later stages of the branch-and-bound process, when good
multiplier estimates have been obtained via RLT or some other relaxation strategy”. Our
approach takes the multipliers, at no cost, from the local solver that already runs at
those stages. It replaces the per-node convex program by the closed-form bound (3), covers
general twice-differentiable functions, and is rigorous.

In BARON, the multipliers of the relaxation are used for optimality-based domain reduction
(Ryoo and Sahinidis 1996). Extending our bound to reduce variable ranges with the same
tangent is a natural next step.

Certifying convexity

Deciding whether every matrix in a symmetric interval matrix is positive semidefinite is
NP-hard, so rigorous solvers rely on sufficient conditions. Examples include
Gerschgorin-type and comparison-matrix tests, midpoint–radius conditions such as
λ_(min)(H_(c)) ≥ ρ(H_(Δ)), and their parametric refinements (Hladík 2018).

Symbolic approaches propagate convexity through the expression graph (Fourer et al. 2010).
Kearfott, Castille, and Tyagi (2013) identify the (possibly intermediate) variables
responsible for nonconvexity, aiming at a non-adaptive subdivision of only those
variables. In interval branch and bound, the reverse use of the interval Hessian is
classical: the nonconvexity test discards boxes on which the objective cannot have an
interior minimizer (Hansen and Walster 2004).

These analyses address the convexity of the problem functions. Ours concerns the
Lagrangian with fixed multipliers on one box, and it is numerical and local. A symbolic
analysis could complement it by predicting on which instances the certification is likely
to succeed.
