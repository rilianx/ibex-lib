\begin{abstract}
Interval branch-and-bound solvers compute rigorous enclosures of the solutions
of nonlinear systems and of global optima. Their polytope-hull contractor
bounds each of the $n$ variables of a node over a linear relaxation by solving
$2n$ small linear programs (LPs) with a floating-point LP solver, whose dual
solutions are certified afterwards by the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound. That
bound is valid for any multipliers, so any LP solver is safe to use; yet a general-purpose solver treats each of the $2n$ LPs as a new
problem. We present a self-certifying simplex (SC) dedicated to the pass: it
solves the $2n$ LPs in sequence on one basis, updating only what changes from
one LP to the next. The certificate makes this simple: any basis yields a valid bound and can
be made dual feasible, so SC never verifies, refactorizes before an optimum
or runs a phase~1. A ladder of configurations in Ibex, from SoPlex as Ibex uses it to SC,
attributes the gain to each step. On recorded passes, from the same boxes and relaxations, a pass of SC on
relaxations of up to 30 rows costs 0.39--0.42 times that of SoPlex as Ibex
uses it, and 20--25\% less than the simplex of SoPlex driven directly, with
the same contraction. Inside the search, the optimizer takes about 20\% less CPU time than with
SoPlex as Ibex uses it. On larger relaxations SC is slower, and a hybrid that chooses the engine by
the size of the relaxation keeps the best of both.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Interval branch-and-bound solvers compute, with mathematical certainty, an
enclosure of all solutions of a system of nonlinear equations and inequalities,
or the global optimum of a constrained problem~\cite{neumaier2004complete}.
Their efficiency depends on \emph{contractors}, operators that shrink the
current box without losing solutions~\cite{chabert2009contractor}. Among them,
contractors based on linear relaxations are among the most effective: the
constraints are linearized over the box, by convex interval
Taylor forms~\cite{araya2012xnewton} or affine
arithmetic~\cite{ninin2015affine,araya2025hybridizing}, and each variable is
minimized and maximized over the resulting polytope. In Ibex this is the
polytope-hull contractor, the linear part of X-Newton, which we introduced
in~\cite{araya2012xnewton}; we later combined the affine and Taylor-based
relaxations~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}.

Each call solves up to $2n$ linear programs, and the rigor of the result cannot
come from the LP solver, which works in floating point. Current implementations
therefore treat the LP solver as a black box and certify its output a
posteriori: the dual solution is turned into a safe bound by the
Neumaier--Shcherbina procedure~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, and an infeasibility
claim is accepted only if the returned Farkas ray passes the same verification.
This design has a cost that is easy to miss: the $2n$ LPs of a node are small
and closely related, typically two or three pivots apart, and each is a
separate call to a general-purpose solver, which prepares the LP as a new
problem, rebuilding its vectors, and refactorizes the basis and checks its
solution before declaring an optimum; on these LPs that work is a large part
of the cost of a call
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

The certificate already makes any LP solver safe to use: with the relaxation
written in a homogeneous form, with one interval-bounded slack per row,
\emph{any} multiplier vector yields a valid bound on the box and on every
sub-box, whatever solver produced it. A black box cannot take advantage of
this, because it treats each call as a new problem. We design the simplex for
the pass instead: the relaxation is loaded once, and the $2n$ LPs are solved
in sequence on one basis, keeping the factorization and updating only what
the new objective and the contracted box change. The certificate is what
makes such a simplex simple. Its multipliers never need to be correct, so it
performs no verification of its own and does not refactorize before
declaring an optimum; and every basis can be made dual feasible by relocating
its nonbasic variables, so no LP of the pass, and no LP after the box
contracts, needs a phase~1 (the search for a feasible starting basis). We call
this simplex \emph{self-certifying} (SC). Against the simplex of SoPlex driven
directly, which also keeps its basis from one LP to the next, we find that
what SC saves is mostly the preparation of each LP as a new problem: switching
off the refactorization and the checks of SoPlex recovers little. How much it
is worth depends on the size of the relaxation and on what else the
contractor does, and measuring that is part of the contribution.

\paragraph{Positioning.} The certificate itself is not new: it is the safe
bound of Neumaier and Shcherbina~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, already applied to
filtering with linear
relaxations~\cite{domes2012rigorous,reyes2023preconditioning}. In those
approaches the multipliers are a by-product of an external solver and
certification is a post-processing step. Resuming a dual simplex inside an
interval method goes back to Yamamura and
coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual}, for an LP emptiness test;
SC extends that warm start to the $2n$ bound problems, with rigorous
certification. Section~\ref{sec:background} gives the details.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A simplex dedicated to the pass.} SC solves the $2n$ problems of
a node in sequence on one basis, without preparing each as a new problem,
without a phase~1, and without the verification and the refactorization
before an optimum of a general-purpose solver (Section~\ref{sec:simplex}). The properties it relies on, validity of
the certificate for any multipliers, exactness at an optimum and dual
feasibility for free, follow from the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound and LP
duality; we state them in Section~\ref{sec:dfb} because the design rests on
them, not as new results.
\item \emph{An attribution of the gain.} A ladder of configurations inside the
same contractor of Ibex, from SoPlex as Ibex uses it, through the merging of
twin rows (a simple preprocessing that halves the relaxation of square systems
for every engine), a tuned SoPlex and its simplex driven directly, to SC and a
hybrid, measures what each step contributes, separately on optimization problems and systems of equations and
on small and large relaxations: on a bank of recorded passes, where every
rung starts from the same boxes and relaxations, and inside the search, with one instance per family
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \emph{The boundary of the approach.} SC pays on small relaxations, where
the fixed cost of a call dominates (per pass, up to about 30 rows); beyond,
the pivots dominate, and SoPlex makes them cheaper by switching to a row
representation of the basis that SC does not have. We measure that
explanation, and give a hybrid contractor
that chooses the engine per node by the size of the relaxation
(Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Interval solvers and linear relaxations.}
Interval branch and bound alternates bisection with contraction. Ibex, on
which we build, composes constraint propagation (HC4~\cite{benhamou1999revising}),
shaving-based consistencies such as ACID~\cite{neveu2015adaptive}, the interval
Newton operator for square systems~\cite{neumaier1990interval}, and contractors
based on linear relaxations~\cite{chabert2009contractor}, whose relaxation
comes from convex interval Taylor forms~\cite{araya2012xnewton}, from affine
arithmetic~\cite{ninin2015affine}, or from both~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}.
QUAD~\cite{lebbah2005quad} and GloptLab~\cite{domes2012rigorous} use linear
relaxations in the same way.

\paragraph{Rigorous bounds for linear programs.}
A floating-point LP solver returns approximate solutions, and two lines of
work make its results rigorous. The first certifies them a posteriori: any
approximate dual vector yields a safe bound once the residual is evaluated in
interval arithmetic, and an approximate Farkas ray proves infeasibility under
the same verification~\cite{neumaier2004safe,jansson2004rigorous,keil2006computational}.
The second solves the LP exactly, in rational
arithmetic~\cite{applegate2007exact}, or to arbitrary precision by iterative
refinement~\cite{gleixner2016iterative}, as in exact mixed-integer
programming~\cite{cook2013hybrid}. Both treat the LP solver as given; we keep
the a posteriori certificate and change the solver. Domes and
Neumaier~\cite{domes2012rigorous} applied the a posteriori bound to filtering
with linear relaxations; in previous work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning} we
wrote the relaxation in the homogeneous form used here and showed that the
dual solutions of the bound LPs give the optimal preconditioner for an
interval Gauss--Seidel projection, and Gleixner et
al.~\cite{gleixner2017three} reuse the equivalent inequalities in sub-boxes.
In all of them certification is a post-processing step, whose cost leads to
applying it to a few promising variables~\cite{domes2012rigorous} or only
periodically~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}. Minimizing and maximizing
each variable over a relaxation is also the optimization-based bound
tightening of global optimization solvers, where rigor is not
required~\cite{ryoo1996branch,belotti2009branching,gleixner2017three}.

\paragraph{Warm starts in interval methods.}
Yamamura and coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} observed that
the optimal tableau of the parent remains dual feasible after a bisection, and
resume an LP emptiness test by the dual simplex method. The heuristic of
skipping the bounds already attained by a primal point, used by the
polytope-hull contractor of Ibex, comes from Baharev, Achterberg and
R\'ev~\cite{baharev2009distillation}.

\paragraph{Simplex techniques.}
SC uses standard ingredients, described by Koberstein~\cite{koberstein2005dual}
for the dual method: the bounded-variable dual simplex with Harris' ratio
test~\cite{harris1973devex}, and the revised method with a sparse LU
factorization updated by the method of Forrest and
Tomlin~\cite{forrest1972updated}, as in SoPlex~\cite{wunderling1996paralleler}.
What is specific to our setting is their use for $2n$ closely related LPs over
the same constraints, with a certificate attached to every basis.

\section{The contractor}\label{sec:contractor}

This section describes the contractor independently of the engine that
solves its LPs: the relaxation and its certificate
(Section~\ref{sec:dfb}), the pass over the bounds
(Section~\ref{sec:pass}) and the merging of twin rows
(Section~\ref{sec:merge}). Section~\ref{sec:simplex} then describes SC, and
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} compares it with external LP solvers inside this
same contractor. Only SC is new; the rest is the common ground on which the
engines are compared.

\subsection{The homogeneous relaxation and its certificate}\label{sec:dfb}

Let $\iv{x}\in\IR^n$ be the current box. A linearizer returns a matrix
$A\in\R^{m\times n}$ of floating-point numbers and an interval vector
$\iv{b}\in\IR^m$, possibly with infinite bounds, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relax}
x \in \iv{x} \text{ is a solution} \;\Longrightarrow\; A x \in \iv{b}.
\end{equation}
We use the two linearizers of our previous
work~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}: the \emph{affine}
relaxation~\cite{ninin2015affine}, and the \emph{combined} relaxation
(\texttt{compo}), which adds to the affine rows those of
X-Newton~\cite{araya2012xnewton}, one per constraint and corner of the box.
We introduce one slack per row, $s = Ax$, and write the relaxation in the
homogeneous form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:homog}
\Abar z = 0,\qquad z = (x,s)\in \iv{z} := \iv{x}\times\iv{s},\qquad
\Abar := [\,A \;\; -I_m\,],
\end{equation}
where $\iv{s}_i := \iv{b}_i \cap \square(A_{i:}\,\iv{x})$ and
$\square(\cdot)$ denotes evaluation in interval arithmetic. We call
$P(\iv{x}) := \{ z\in\iv{z} : \Abar z = 0\}$ the \emph{relaxation polytope}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:relax}
If $x^*\in\iv{x}$ is a solution, then $(x^*, Ax^*)\in P(\iv{x})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$A_{i:}x^*\in\iv{b}_i$ by~\eqref{eq:relax} and $A_{i:}x^*\in\square(A_{i:}\iv{x})$
by the inclusion property of interval arithmetic; the equation holds by
construction of $s$.
\end{proof}

The certificate combines the $m$ rows of $\Abar z=0$ linearly, with one
\emph{multiplier} per row, into a single equation $\gamma^Tz=0$ that every point
of the polytope satisfies. Any vector of multipliers $\lambda\in\R^m$ gives a
valid equation; the best ones for bounding $z_k$ are the dual solutions of the
LP that minimizes or maximizes $z_k$ over the polytope, and the simplex of Section~\ref{sec:simplex}
produces them.

\begin{definition}[Certificate]\label{def:cert}
For a vector of multipliers $\lambda\in\R^m$, let $\iv{\gamma}\in\IR^{n+m}$ be an interval vector that
contains $\gamma := \Abar^T\lambda$, computed in outward-rounded interval
arithmetic. For $k\in\{1,\dots,n+m\}$ with $0\notin\iv{\gamma}_k$, the
\emph{certified enclosure} of $z_k$ is
\[
\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z}) := -\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j\neq k}\iv{\gamma}_j\,\iv{z}_j\Big) \Big/ \iv{\gamma}_k ,
\]
and $\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z}):=\R$ when $0\in\iv{\gamma}_k$;
and the \emph{residual} is $\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z}) := \sum_{j}\iv{\gamma}_j\,\iv{z}_j$.
\end{definition}

The idea is a single line. Every point $z$ of the polytope satisfies
$\Abar z=0$, hence $\gamma^T z=\lambda^T\Abar z=0$ for any $\lambda$, that is,
$\gamma_k z_k=-\sum_{j\neq k}\gamma_j z_j$. Bounding the right-hand side over
the box in interval arithmetic, and dividing by $\gamma_k$, encloses $z_k$; and
if the residual $\iv{R}$ excludes zero, no point of the box can satisfy
$\gamma^Tz=0$, so the polytope is empty. A contraction step therefore replaces
$\iv{z}_k$ by $\iv{z}_k\cap\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$, and an emptiness test
checks $0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$. All statements below hold for the
floating-point $A$ returned by the linearizer; rounding in $\iv{\gamma}$ and
$\iv{C}_k$ is covered by outward rounding.

\begin{proposition}[Validity]\label{prop:valid}
Let $\lambda\in\R^m$ be arbitrary. For every $z\in P(\iv{x})$ and every $k$
with $0\notin\iv{\gamma}_k$, $z_k\in\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$; if
$0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$, then $P(\iv{x})=\emptyset$ and $\iv{x}$
contains no solution. Both claims hold on every sub-box $\iv{x}'\subseteq\iv{x}$
with the matrix $\Abar$ computed on $\iv{x}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $z\in P(\iv{x})$ and the exact $\gamma=\Abar^T\lambda\in\iv{\gamma}$,
$\gamma^Tz=\lambda^T\Abar z=0$; both claims follow from the inclusion property
of interval arithmetic and Lemma~\ref{lem:relax}. The linearization on
$\iv{x}$ remains valid on $\iv{x}'$, so the polytope over $\iv{x}'$ lies in
$P(\iv{x})$.
\end{proof}

No property of $\lambda$ is used: it need not be optimal, feasible or
accurate, and errors in it can only weaken the enclosure. Written back in the
variables $x$, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$ is the Neumaier--Shcherbina safe
lower bound of $\min x_k$ subject to $Ax\in\iv{b}$, $x\in\iv{x}$, with
approximate dual $\lambda$~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, and the row-wise solution
of the preconditioned system of Domes and Neumaier~\cite[\S5]{domes2012rigorous};
validity on sub-boxes is used by Gleixner et al.~\cite{gleixner2017three} and
in our earlier work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}. The homogeneous form,
introduced in~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}, adds one detail: each slack is
bounded by $\square(A_{i:}\iv{x})$ even when a side of $\iv{b}_i$ is infinite,
so a multiplier of the ``wrong'' sign on a one-sided row gives a weaker but
finite bound, and literally any $\lambda$ certifies. Two standard facts
complete what the design uses. At an optimal basis of $\mathrm{LP}_k^{\min}$ with
$z_k$ basic, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda^*,\iv{z})$ equals the optimal value in
exact arithmetic, by LP duality~\cite[Prop.~1]{reyes2023preconditioning}; in
floating point the excess is at most $\sum_j\wid(\iv{\gamma}_j)\magn(\iv{z}_j)$
plus the effect of the inaccuracy of $\lambda$, as for the
Neumaier--Shcherbina bound. And every basis can be made dual feasible for
free~\cite{koberstein2005dual}:

\begin{proposition}[Dual-feasible start]\label{prop:start}
The slack basis $B=-I_m$ is dual feasible for the bound problem of every
structural variable $z_k$, $k\le n$, with $\iv{z}_k$ bounded. If every
$\iv{z}_j$ is bounded, any basis becomes dual feasible after placing each
nonbasic $z_j$ at its lower bound if its reduced cost $d_j>0$ and at its upper
bound if $d_j<0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With the slack basis the cost of every basic variable is zero, so $\lambda=0$
and $d=e_k$, and $z_k$ at its lower bound (at its upper bound for the maximization) has
the right sign. In general $d$
does not depend on where the nonbasic variables sit, and the relocation
enforces the sign conditions whenever the required bound is finite.
\end{proof}

When the objective changes from one bound to the next, or the box contracts,
relocation thus restores dual feasibility without a phase~1, which is what
lets one basis serve the $2n$ problems; the case of a box change is the warm
start of Yamamura and coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual}.
Section~\ref{sec:simplex} explains how the simplex produces $\lambda$.

\paragraph{Why not a rigorous simplex.}
Since any multipliers give a valid bound, rigor is needed only in the final
evaluation of $\iv{\gamma}$ and $\iv{C}_k$, not in the pivoting. Pivoting in
interval arithmetic pays the width growth of interval Gaussian
elimination~\cite{neumaier1990interval}, and solving each LP exactly or to high
precision~\cite{applegate2007exact,gleixner2016iterative} buys an exactness
that the bound does not need; an early version of our contractor that pivoted
in interval arithmetic was 2.8 times slower, with the same contraction\todo{decir sobre qu\'e
instancias se midi\'o}.

\subsection{One pass over the bounds}\label{sec:pass}

At each node the relaxation is computed, written in the
form~\eqref{eq:homog}, and loaded once. The contractor then makes one pass over
the up to $2n$ bound problems
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpk}
\mathrm{LP}_k^{\sigma}:\quad \sigma\; z_k \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Abar z = 0,\; z\in\iv{z},
\qquad k\le n,\ \sigma\in\{\min,\max\},
\end{equation}
and we write $\mathrm{LP}_k$ when the sense does not matter.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pass} gives the pass. The set $L$ holds the bounds still
to solve (line~\ref{ap:init}). Each LP is warm-started from the final basis of
the previous one, and the engine returns the multipliers of the basis where it
stops (line~\ref{ap:solve}). They certify the bound once, and the box is
contracted before the next LP starts (line~\ref{ap:contract}), so every later
LP works on the smaller box. If the residual $\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$ excludes
zero, the node is empty and the pass stops (line~\ref{ap:empty}).

No other outcome stops the pass, because any multipliers certify
(Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}): an LP that stops at its iteration limit, or
reports an infeasibility whose ray fails the residual test, still contracts
with the multipliers it returns, if less than an optimal basis would. The
polytope-hull contractor of Ibex~2.8.9 instead abandons the pass in both
cases; we corrected it, the correction has been submitted to the Ibex
distribution, and every configuration of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} uses
it, including the one that stands for Ibex. Ibex limits SoPlex to 100
iterations per LP, and every engine of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, SC
included, uses that limit.

Line~\ref{ap:next} chooses the next bound with two rules of the
polytope-hull contractor of Ibex~\cite{baharev2009distillation}, which use the
primal point of the last LP solved to optimality. A bound already attained by
such a point is \emph{skipped}: it is removed from $L$ without solving its LP,
which would return that value, and the pass ends when every remaining bound is
skipped. Among the others, the next one is the bound nearest to the last
primal point, whose optimum lies closest to the vertex where the previous LP
ended, so that it needs fewer pivots.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One pass over the bounds of a node. \textsc{Solve} is the LP engine,
SC (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sc}) or an external solver.}\label{alg:pass}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require relaxation $\Abar z=0$ with twin rows merged, box $\iv{z}$
\Ensure contracted box $\iv{z}$, or $\emptyset$
\State $L\gets\{(k,\sigma): k\le n,\ \sigma\in\{\min,\max\}\}$\label{ap:init}
\While{$L\neq\emptyset$}
  \State $(k,\sigma)\gets$ next bound of $L$; remove it from $L$
    \Comment{skipping and order}\label{ap:next}
  \State $\lambda\gets\textsc{Solve}(\mathrm{LP}_k^{\sigma},\iv{z})$
    \Comment{warm start, at most 100 iterations}\label{ap:solve}
  \If{$0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$} \Return $\emptyset$
     \Comment{Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}}\label{ap:empty}
  \EndIf
  \State $\iv{z}_k\gets\iv{z}_k\cap\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$
    \Comment{Neumaier--Shcherbina bound~\cite{neumaier2004safe}}\label{ap:contract}
\EndWhile
\State \Return $\iv{z}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Merging twin rows}\label{sec:merge}

Rows of the relaxation that are proportional, within a relative tolerance of
$10^{-9}$, are merged into one row whose slack interval is the intersection of
their scaled intervals; the polytope does not change. The affine linearizer
writes each equation as two one-sided rows with the same normal, so merging
halves $m$ on square systems, and \texttt{compo} writes some rows twice, with opposite signs, far
apart in the matrix. Merging is done once per node, before any LP, and gives a
smaller LP to SC and to an external solver alike.

\section{A simplex for the \texorpdfstring{$2n$}{2n} bound problems}\label{sec:simplex}

For each structural variable $x_k$ the pass of Section~\ref{sec:pass} needs
the two bounds of $\mathrm{LP}_k$~\eqref{eq:lpk}. We write $[l_j,u_j]:=\iv{z}_j$.
A basis is a set of $m$ columns of $\Abar$ forming a nonsingular matrix $B$;
$B_r$ is the variable basic in row $r$, $x_B$ the vector of values of the basic
variables, $N$ the set of nonbasic variables and $\Abar_N$ their columns. With
cost vector $c=\pm e_k$, the
multipliers are $\lambda^T = c_B^T B^{-1}$ and the reduced costs
$d = c - \Abar^T\lambda$. For the bounded-variable simplex, a basis with each
nonbasic variable at one of its bounds is \emph{dual feasible} if $d_j\ge 0$ for
nonbasic variables at their lower bound and $d_j\le 0$ for those at their upper
bound, and \emph{primal feasible} if the basic variables lie within their
bounds. This section describes how the simplex solves the $2n$ problems of a
node; the design choices are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{A simplex for one pass.} SC is not a general LP solver. It
solves the $2n$ LPs of one pass over a relaxation loaded once, in sequence and
on one basis: between two LPs it keeps its factorization and recomputes only
what the new objective and the contracted box change. The certificate is
what makes this simple. Since it is valid for any $\lambda$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}), the simplex never has to return a correct
solution, only a good basis. A
general-purpose LP solver, before declaring an optimum, refactorizes the basis
to remove the error accumulated by the updates of its factorization,
recomputes its vectors and verifies the solution, and the contractor then
verifies it once more through the certificate. SC uses the final basis as it
is: an inaccurate $\lambda$ yields a weaker bound, never an invalid one, so the
error of the factorization is controlled only as far as pivoting requires.
Likewise, since every basis can be made dual feasible
(Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), no phase~1 is needed when the objective changes
or the box contracts, and the $2n$ problems share one basis; with the primal
warm start of Section~\ref{sec:warm} none is needed either, because the
previous optimal basis stays primal feasible. Section~\ref{sec:bank} measures
what this design is worth: against the simplex of SoPlex driven directly, the
gain comes mostly from not preparing each LP as a new problem, more than from
the omitted checks.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:sc} shows one call of SC, the \textsc{Solve} of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pass}. Lines~\ref{as:slack}--\ref{as:xb} prepare the LP
from the state left by the previous one (Section~\ref{sec:warm}). The loop of
lines~\ref{as:loop}--\ref{as:limit} combines both methods: it takes a dual step
while some basic variable violates its bounds, a primal step while some reduced
cost has the wrong sign, and stops at an optimal basis
(line~\ref{as:opt}), at a Farkas ray (line~\ref{as:ray}) or at the iteration
limit (line~\ref{as:limit}); Section~\ref{sec:iter} describes the steps. After a dual warm start
the basis is dual feasible and only dual steps occur; after a primal one it is
primal feasible and only primal steps occur. The two lines marked $\star$ are
where the design described above departs from a general-purpose solver:
line~\ref{as:keep}, the warm start, starts each LP from the final basis of
the previous one, with its factors,
and line~\ref{as:opt} returns the basis as it is. The certificate is computed
by the caller, in lines~\ref{ap:empty}--\ref{ap:contract} of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pass} (Section~\ref{sec:certif}).

\begin{algorithm}[!tp]
\caption{\textsc{Solve} in SC: one LP of the pass. The relaxation is loaded
once per node; the basis and its LU factors persist from one call to the next.
Comments marked $\star$ point to the work a general-purpose solver does
differently.}\label{alg:sc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require bound $(k,\sigma)$, with $c=e_k$ to minimize and $c=-e_k$ to maximize;
  box $\iv{z}$, contracted since the previous call; warm start, primal or dual
  (Section~\ref{sec:warm})
\If{first LP of the node}
  \State $B\gets -I_m$, factorized; each nonbasic $z_j$ at its lower bound
    \Comment{Prop.~\ref{prop:start}}\label{as:slack}
\Else
  \State $B\gets$ final basis of the previous LP, with its LU factors
    \Comment{$\star$ a solver starts anew}\label{as:keep}
\EndIf
\State $\lambda\gets B^{-T}c_B$;\ \ $d\gets c-\Abar^T\lambda$
  \Comment{multipliers and reduced costs for $c$}\label{as:lam}
\If{first LP \textbf{or} dual warm start}
  \State move each nonbasic $z_j$ whose $d_j$ has the wrong sign to its other bound\label{as:reloc}
\EndIf
\State $x_B\gets -B^{-1}\Abar_N z_N$
  \Comment{values of the basic variables}\label{as:xb}
\For{$t=1,\dots,100$}\label{as:loop}
  \If{some basic variable violates its bounds} \Comment{dual step}\label{as:dual}
    \State $r\gets$ the basic variable with the largest violation;
      $\delta\gets x_{B_r}$ minus the bound it violates
    \State $\rho_r\gets B^{-T}e_r$;\ \ $\alpha_r^T\gets\rho_r^T\Abar$
    \State $q\gets$ ratio test on $d_j/\alpha_{rj}$ with Harris' rule
    \If{no $q$ exists} \Return $\lambda\gets\rho_r$
      \Comment{Farkas ray}\label{as:ray}
    \EndIf
    \State $\alpha_q\gets B^{-1}\Abar_{:q}$;\ \ $\theta_p\gets\delta/\alpha_{rq}$\label{as:dualend}
  \ElsIf{some nonbasic $d_j$ has the wrong sign} \Comment{primal step}\label{as:primal}
    \State $q\gets$ the nonbasic variable with the largest such $|d_j|$;
      $\alpha_q\gets B^{-1}\Abar_{:q}$
    \State $\theta_p\gets$ the largest step of $z_q$, of the sign of $-d_q$, that keeps
      $x_B$ within bounds
    \State $r\gets$ the basic variable that reaches its bound at $\theta_p$
      \Comment{stable entries of $\alpha_q$ only}
    \If{$z_q$ reaches its other bound first}
      \State $x_B\gets x_B-(u_q-l_q)\,\mathrm{sign}(-d_q)\,\alpha_q$; move $z_q$ to that bound
      \State \textbf{go to} the next iteration \Comment{bound flip, no pivot}
    \EndIf
    \State $\rho_r\gets B^{-T}e_r$;\ \ $\alpha_r^T\gets\rho_r^T\Abar$\label{as:primalr}
  \Else
    \State \Return $\lambda$
      \Comment{optimal; $\star$ a solver refactorizes and verifies first}\label{as:opt}
  \EndIf
  \State $\theta_d\gets d_q/\alpha_{rq}$
  \State $x_B\gets x_B-\theta_p\alpha_q$;\ \ $\lambda\gets\lambda+\theta_d\rho_r$;\ \
    $d\gets d-\theta_d\alpha_r$\label{as:upd}
  \State $B_r\gets q$;\ \ $x_{B_r}\gets z_q+\theta_p$
    \Comment{the leaving variable stays at its bound}
  \State update the LU factors of $B$ \Comment{Forrest--Tomlin}
  \If{50 pivots since the last factorization} refactorize $B$\label{as:pivot} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\lambda$ \Comment{iteration limit; $\lambda$ still certifies}\label{as:limit}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Starting and warm-starting}\label{sec:warm}

The first LP of a pass starts from the slack basis $B=-I_m$
(line~\ref{as:slack} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:sc}), which is dual feasible for
every $\mathrm{LP}_k$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), and is solved by the
dual method, with no phase~1: the costs of the slacks are zero, so $\lambda=0$
and $d=c$ (line~\ref{as:lam}); every nonbasic variable starts at its lower
bound, and for a maximization line~\ref{as:reloc} moves $z_k$ to its upper
bound. Between two consecutive LPs the objective
changes and the contracted bound changes the box. SC keeps the basis and its
factors (line~\ref{as:keep}) and recomputes $\lambda$ and $d$ for the new
objective (line~\ref{as:lam}); two warm starts are then possible. The
\emph{dual} one relocates every nonbasic variable to the bound indicated by the
sign of its new reduced cost (line~\ref{as:reloc}), which restores dual
feasibility, and continues with the dual method; the \emph{primal} one keeps
the nonbasic variables where they are and reoptimizes with primal steps, as an
LP solver does. Under the dual method the
certificate is available at every iteration, and it never gets worse:

\begin{proposition}[Monotone certified bound]\label{prop:mono}
Run the dual simplex method on $\mathrm{LP}_k^{\min}$ from a dual-feasible basis. At
every iteration $t$ in which $z_k$ is basic, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda_t,\iv{z})$
is a valid lower bound on $z_k$ over $P(\iv{x})$ and, in exact arithmetic, it
is nondecreasing in $t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Validity is Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}. With $z_k$ basic, $d=e_k-\Abar^T\lambda_t$
has $d_k=0$, so $\gamma_k=1$, $\gamma_j=-d_j$ for $j\neq k$, and
$\inff\iv{C}_k=\sum_{j\neq k}\min_{z_j\in\iv{z}_j}d_jz_j=\min_{z\in\iv{z}}d^Tz$,
the Lagrangian dual function of $\mathrm{LP}_k$ at $\lambda_t$, which the
iterates of the dual simplex method do not decrease.
\end{proof}

Both warm starts reach an optimal basis and give the same bound; they differ
in cost. A nonbasic variable that must move to its opposite bound costs the
primal warm start an iteration; relocation moves it without one, but changes
the values of the basic variables, which may then violate their bounds and
cost the dual method its own iterations. On systems of equations every
row is a narrow slab (its slack interval covers 13\% of the range of the row
over the box, on average), and a median of 1.9 nonbasic variables per LP
change side, against 0.3 on optimization problems, whose slack intervals cover
48\% of the range\todo{decir d\'onde se midi\'o: banco de la
Secci\'on~\ref{sec:bank}?}. SC therefore uses the primal warm start in the optimizer,
where the dual one is 4\% slower, and the dual one on systems of equations,
where both cost about the same; with \texttt{compo}, where neither wins on
every system, it alternates the two on the first nodes and keeps the one with
the lower measured CPU time per LP, trying the other again every 25 nodes
(so the choice, unlike the rest of SC, depends on timing).

\subsection{Simplex iterations}\label{sec:iter}

We follow the bounded-variable simplex of~\cite{koberstein2005dual}. Each
iteration exchanges one basic and one nonbasic variable using the pivot column
$\alpha_q = B^{-1}\Abar_{:q}$ of the entering variable (FTRAN), the pivot row
$\alpha_r^T = \rho_r^T\Abar$ with $B^T\rho_r=e_r$ (BTRAN), and updates
$x_B \leftarrow x_B - \theta_p\alpha_q$,
$\lambda \leftarrow \lambda + \theta_d\rho_r$ and
$d \leftarrow d - \theta_d\alpha_r$ (line~\ref{as:upd} of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sc}), so that $\lambda$, which the certificate needs, is
always at hand.

\paragraph{Dual steps.} In a dual iteration
(lines~\ref{as:dual}--\ref{as:dualend} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:sc}) the basis is
dual feasible and some basic variable violates its bounds. The leaving variable is the basic
variable with the largest violation. The entering variable comes from the
ratio test over the pivot row: among the nonbasic $z_j$ whose reduced cost
would change sign in the wrong direction, the one with the smallest
$|d_j/\alpha_{rj}|$ keeps the basis dual feasible; among the candidates within
a relative band of that ratio we take the largest $|\alpha_{rj}|$ (Harris'
rule), which avoids tiny pivots. If no entering variable exists, the LP is
infeasible and $\rho_r$ is a Farkas ray (line~\ref{as:ray}).

\paragraph{Rules left out.} We also implemented the standard refinements of
the dual method~\cite{koberstein2005dual}: devex
pricing~\cite{harris1973devex} for the leaving variable and the long-step (bound
flipping) ratio test~\cite{maros2003generalized}. On average
they did not pay (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), and SC uses the textbook
rules above: on the small LPs of a pass, the choice of pivots matters less
than the work that surrounds them.

\paragraph{Primal steps, tolerances.} Primal steps
(lines~\ref{as:primal}--\ref{as:primalr}) enter the nonbasic
variable with the largest reduced cost of the wrong sign (Dantzig's rule;
with \texttt{compo}, whose primal warm starts are longer, devex weights);
pivots below a stability threshold are filtered out before choosing the
leaving variable, since without this filter a rejected pivot was
indistinguishable from optimality. If the entering variable reaches its other
bound before any basic variable reaches one, it moves there without a pivot (a
bound flip). The feasibility tolerances are $10^{-9}$,
relative to the value tested, and $10^{-6}$ with \texttt{compo} on systems of
equations, whose nearly parallel rows make the LPs degenerate. A tolerance is a
matter of efficiency, not of rigor: too wide, it weakens the contraction; too
tight, the warm-started simplex reports infeasibility on boxes that are not
empty, the ray fails the residual test, and SC solves that LP again from the
slack basis.

\paragraph{Factorization.} SC keeps a sparse LU factorization of $B$, computed by the factorization code
of SoPlex and updated after each pivot by the Forrest--Tomlin
method~\cite{forrest1972updated}, which reuses the FTRAN of the entering column.
It refactorizes every 50 pivots (line~\ref{as:pivot}) and recomputes $x_B$
every 32, to limit drift.
With one slack per row, the basis of SC has dimension $m$, the number of rows:
SC works in what SoPlex calls the column representation.

\subsection{Certification}\label{sec:certif}

When an LP terminates, the certificate $\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$ is computed
once in outward-rounded interval arithmetic, over the rows with
$\lambda_i\neq 0$, and $\iv{C}_k$ and $\iv{R}$ follow in $O(n+m)$. When a dual
iteration finds a violated basic variable and no entering variable in its row
$r$, $\lambda=\rho_r$ is a Farkas ray, and the residual test turns it into a
proof of emptiness. Certifying a bound costs the same as the
Neumaier--Shcherbina post-processing of an external solver's dual
(1.3--3.1\,$\mu$s per LP on three optimization problems); the difference
between the approaches is not the cost of a certificate but how $\lambda$ is
produced: by a call that treats each LP as a new problem, or by one simplex
that serves the whole pass.

\section{Experimental evaluation}\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{A ladder of configurations.}
All configurations run inside the \emph{same} contractor: the same pass over
the $2n$ bounds, with the same skipping rule, the same certificate
$\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$ applied to whatever multipliers the engine returns
(its dual solution, or its Farkas ray if it reports infeasibility), and the
same policy when an LP ends without an optimum: the multipliers where it
stopped certify a weaker bound, and the pass continues
(Section~\ref{sec:pass}). The configurations differ in one step at a time:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[R0] \emph{SoPlex as Ibex uses it}: SoPlex~4.0.2 through the LP interface
of Ibex, which stores the $n$ variable bounds as rows, with presolving on, the
tolerance $10^{-9}$, the nearest bound first as in the contractor of Ibex, and
at most 100 iterations per LP;
\item[R1] \emph{+ merging twin rows} (Section~\ref{sec:merge});
\item[R2] \emph{+ a tuned SoPlex}: its own instance holding the box as column
bounds, presolving off, the tolerance $10^{-6}$ for systems of equations and
$10^{-9}$ for optimization;%
\footnote{Each of these choices is the best one for SoPlex on these LPs:
presolving each new relaxation costs more than it saves on LPs of a few
pivots, $10^{-9}$ makes SoPlex report spurious infeasibility on relaxations
with nearly equal rows, and $10^{-6}$ weakens its dual on optimization
problems. Turning off scaling, or forcing the row or column representation or
the primal algorithm, did not help, and SoPlex~7.1.6 was 1.2--1.9 times slower
than 4.0.2 on a sample of eight problems.}
\item[R3] \emph{the bare simplex}: the simplex of SoPlex driven directly through
its \texttt{SPxSolver} class, the LP kept loaded, only the objective and the
column bounds changed between LPs, without the checks, copies and
unscaling of the \texttt{SoPlex} class; \texttt{SPxSolver} itself still
refactorizes before declaring an optimum and checks the precision of its
solution. It is the cheapest way to use SoPlex. As in R2, the rule of SoPlex chooses the representation of the
basis: by columns (dimension $m$), or by rows (dimension $n$) when the
relaxation has more than 1.2 rows per variable;
\item[R4] \emph{SC} (Section~\ref{sec:simplex});%
\footnote{On systems with the affine relaxation SC keeps the tolerance
$10^{-9}$ while R2 and R3 use $10^{-6}$. With $10^{-6}$ a pass of SC costs the
same (0.84 instead of 0.85 times that of the bare simplex, geometric mean over
the 36 systems) and contracts 1\% less.}
\item[R5] \emph{the hybrid}: SC on relaxations of at most 30 rows, the bare
simplex beyond.
\end{enumerate}
Each rung is compared with R0. HiGHS~1.15.1 and Gurobi~12.0.3, with one
thread, the dual simplex, the basis kept between LPs and no presolve, cost
2.3--4.6 times as much per pass as the tuned SoPlex, so we leave them out of
the ladder.

\paragraph{Setting.}
Contractor and solvers are implemented in Ibex 2.8.9, with the affine and
\texttt{compo} relaxations of Section~\ref{sec:dfb}. Optimization
problems use the affine relaxation, since in preliminary experiments
\texttt{compo} cost more than it saved with every engine; systems of
equations are evaluated with both, since \texttt{compo} is the better one on
large systems~\cite{araya2025hybridizing} and the affine one keeps most
relaxations small.
Per node, after merging, the affine relaxation has a median of 5 rows
(quartiles 3--9) on optimization problems and 16 (9--30) on systems of
equations; \texttt{compo} has 56 (36--94) on systems. We call an instance, with a given
relaxation, \emph{small} if its merged relaxation has at most 30 rows on
average over the nodes, and \emph{large} otherwise; these are the two size
classes of the tables. For systems of
equations we use the solver composition of Ibex (HC4, ACID, interval Newton
when the system is square, and the linear contractor within a fixed point with
HC4) with the LSmear bisector, on the 242 systems of the Ibex solver benchmark
that some configuration solves within 3600\,s, 2 seeds, 60\,s per run; for
optimization, IbexOpt on the 240 problems of the Ibex optimization benchmark
that some configuration solves within 3600\,s, 4 seeds, 60\,s per run. Three
of them, bearing, alkyl and alkylbis, are left out of the means of the solver
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). All
runs were made on two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4 processors (2.1\,GHz, 16 cores)
with 64\,GB of memory, under Linux 6.17, with at most 6\,GB per run, eight
or fourteen runs at a time (the runs of R0 and R1 inside the search were made
at eight; the ratio of R2 to R1 is the same against them as against runs of
R2 made at eight); the code is compiled with GCC 13.3 (\texttt{-O3}) with the
GAOL~4.2.0 interval library. CPU times are process times. The code, the
banks of passes and the scripts are public (see the statement of code and
data availability).

\paragraph{Rigor.}
SC is rigorous by Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}; we also checked it against the
LP hull. On four systems, for each of the 2\,834 bounds where SC contracted more
than the polytope-hull contractor of Ibex on the same box and polytope, we
solved the bound LP over the input box with a fresh instance of SoPlex; SC
attained this hull in 98\% of these bounds and went beyond it in 19, all within
the $10^{-9}$ feasibility tolerance of the oracle.

\subsection{Cost of a pass}\label{sec:bank}

To measure the contractor without the noise of the search, we built a bank of
passes: for 110 instances, 36 systems of equations (one per family) and 74
optimization problems (one per family and size class, among those on which R0
takes more than 1\,s), we recorded the input boxes of 1\,000 calls of the
contractor spread along a search with SC, with the affine relaxation, and
replayed every call with each rung from the same input box and relaxation. Because the affine
relaxation of only six systems exceeds 30 rows, a second bank does the same
with \texttt{compo}, the best relaxation on large systems, for 41 systems
whose relaxation has more than 30 rows (31 to 687, median 63; one per family,
R0 above 1\,s). The 74 optimization
problems do not count three that were left out: on pentagon and ex8\_2\_4,
SoPlex as Ibex uses it aborted with an internal exception, and on concon the
affine linearization failed for every rung. Two choices of the bank should be
kept in mind: its boxes are those visited by a search with SC, although every
rung replays them from the same input, and its instances are those on which
R0 is slow enough to be measured.

On optimization problems and on systems of up to 30 rows, a pass of SC costs
0.39--0.42 times that of R0, and 25\% and 20\% less than the bare simplex
(SC is cheaper on 70 of the 74 optimization problems and 25 of the 30
systems). The hybrid differs from SC on one optimization problem, of 33
rows. Before SC, merging saves 7--16\%, tuning SoPlex 21--27\% of what
remains, and driving its simplex directly 23--24\% more. The median pass on optimization
problems takes 264\,$\mu$s with R0, 136 with the bare simplex and 88 with SC;
on small systems, 199, 109 and 85\,$\mu$s (medians weigh the many fast
instances, where SC gains most).

The gain of SC lies in the cost of an LP, not in its pivots. The tuned SoPlex
and the bare simplex need a median of 1.8 pivots per LP, SC 2.8, because
SoPlex often ends an LP at a vertex that is already optimal for the next bound
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); yet an LP of SC costs 11.6\,$\mu$s on
optimization problems and 16.3 on small systems, against 16.2 and 21.6 for
the bare simplex. What SC does not do is what makes the difference. We measured
it against the tuned SoPlex (R2), whose calls perform all of that work:
re-inserting into SC, at every LP, the work of a call of the tuned SoPlex,
on separate vectors so that the contraction does not
change, brings a pass of SC from 0.55--0.60 to 0.88--0.92 times that of R2
(geometric means on 100 of the 104 instances; the emulation failed on four):
refactorizing at every LP accounts for 49--56\% of the gap (in logarithm),
verifying the solution for 22--23\%, and recomputing the vectors and copying
the solution for 7--8\%. Against the bare simplex (R3) the picture changes, since R3 already skips
the checks and copies of the \texttt{SoPlex} class. Its own refactorization
before an optimum and precision check are not what separates it from SC:
with two switches added to \texttt{SPxSolver} that skip them, the
factorizations per LP drop from 0.79 to 0.10 on ex7\_2\_3, yet a pass of the
bare simplex becomes only 7\% cheaper on the optimization problems and 4\%
on the systems, with the same contraction, and SC remains 22\% and
12\% cheaper (geometric means; it wins on 69 of the 74 problems and 22 of the
36 systems). On bases of 5 to 16 rows a factorization is cheap. What remains
is the cost of a call of \texttt{SPxSolver::solve} as such: it prepares every
LP as a new problem, rebuilding its primal and dual vectors and its pricing
and ratio-test vectors from the factorization (4--25\% of its time in profiles
of four problems), and runs a per-pivot bookkeeping built for general LPs. SC
is built for the $2n$ LPs of a pass: between two LPs it recomputes the multipliers and reduced costs for the new
objective, and the basic values when the dual warm start relocates nonbasic
variables, and keeps its factorization.

\paragraph{Merging pays more with SC.}
Ordered as a ladder, each step is measured on the engine of its rung, and
merging is measured on R0, the most expensive one. On the same bank, merging
saves 7\% of a pass of R0 on optimization problems and 16\% on systems of
equations, but 21\% and 44--47\% of a pass of SC. A call of SoPlex carries a
fixed cost that does not depend on the rows; SC has almost none, so its time
follows the size of the LP, and halving the rows of a square system nearly
halves it. Merging is a simple preprocessing, not a contribution of this
paper, but it is worth most with an engine that has removed the fixed cost of
a call.

\paragraph{Same contraction.}
All rungs empty the same boxes and contract alike: the sum of the widths of
the box shrinks by 25.5\% per call on average on optimization problems and
by 18.6--18.7\% on small systems for R1 to R4, and
within 1\% on all but 6--9 of the 110 instances. The exception is R0, which
ends 8.5\% of the LPs of small systems at its limit of 100 iterations
(0.9\% after merging; the tuned SoPlex, with the same limit, almost none); the
multipliers where it stops certify a weaker bound, and R0 contracts by 18.2\%
(on Geneig-icse, the extreme case, by 23\% against 34\%). On the \texttt{compo}
bank the LPs are larger and more degenerate, and with the tolerance of
$10^{-6}$ the certified bounds of the engines differ slightly more: SC
contracts by 15.6\%, the tuned SoPlex by 15.9\% and the bare simplex by 16.1\%,
while the limit of R0 ends 5\% of the LPs without an optimum.

\paragraph{Large relaxations.}
On the systems whose relaxation exceeds 30 rows the order of the last two
rungs is reversed (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}): on the \texttt{compo} bank a
pass of SC costs 1.39 times that of the bare simplex (1.42 in geometric mean)
and is cheaper on only 4 of the 41 systems, and on the six affine ones it
costs 1.49 times (geometric mean) and loses on all. Merging and tuning SoPlex keep their
weight; the hybrid hands these relaxations to the bare simplex.

\paragraph{Why SC is slower there.}
What SC saves is a fixed cost per LP, and on these relaxations an LP needs a
dozen pivots instead of two or three, so the saving no longer weighs. What
remains is the cost of the pivots, and three different things happen there.

\emph{The representation of the basis} is the main one. A vertex of the
relaxation can be described by a basis of dimension $m$, with one basic column
per row, or by a basis of dimension $n$, the $n$ rows and bounds that are
tight. SC, with one slack per row, always works with the first. SoPlex
chooses: by columns when the relaxation has at most 1.2 rows per variable, by
rows otherwise. With \texttt{compo} the rows outnumber the variables several
times (147 rows for 35 variables on Fourbar-icse), so the bare simplex
factorizes and solves with a basis of dimension $n$ where SC uses one of
dimension $m$: with almost the same pivots (1.05 times), SC spends there three
times as long in the triangular solves of the entering column. To measure how
much of the gap this explains, we forced the bare simplex to the column
representation on the \texttt{compo} bank. It becomes 1.35 times slower
(geometric mean), with the same contraction, and a pass of SC then costs 1.04
times that of the bare simplex instead of 1.41 in the same runs, and is cheaper on 18 of the 41
systems; on the systems without the two difficulties below (the
Redeco and Eco families, Katsura-30, Dietmaier, Directkin, Butcher) the two are
within 10\% of each other. The representation thus accounts for almost 90\%
of the gap, in logarithm. On the bank of the affine relaxation, where
merging leaves about as many rows as variables, SoPlex already works by
columns, and forcing it to rows makes it slower (a pass of SC goes from 0.85
to 0.79 times that of the bare simplex).

\emph{Stalling} remains on a few systems: on Katsurabis-14, 17 of the
16\,562 LPs of SC, degenerate ones, pivot until the limit of 100 iterations,
while the bare simplex reaches an optimum on all of its LPs; the primal steps
of SC have no protection against stalling.

\emph{The systems with band structure} (Bratu, BroydenTri, BroydenBanded,
DiscreteBoundary) are the third case: there SC makes several times the pivots
of the bare simplex, up to eleven times on the six affine systems above 30
rows, where both engines work by columns. Devex pricing and the long-step ratio
test (Section~\ref{sec:iter}) help on them with the affine relaxation: they
bring a pass of SC from 0.71 to 0.55 times that of R0 on those six systems
(the bare simplex: 0.47), but leave it at 1.51 times the bare simplex on the
\texttt{compo} bank (geometric mean), and change little on the smaller
relaxations. We have no explanation for these systems beyond the pivoting
rules of SoPlex, its automatic pricer with the bound-flipping ratio test.

systems). The total time of the hybrid is flat for $T$ between 15 and 30, and
we use $T=30$; the contractor applies it per node, with the size of the
relaxation of the previous node. Run inside the search on the systems of
equations with \texttt{compo} (two seeds), the hybrid with $T=15$, 20 and 30
costs 0.98--1.00 times the bare simplex on the small systems and 0.98 on the
large ones, where SC alone costs 1.15: the choice per node loses nothing, and
the threshold does not matter inside this range.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why SC wins.} SC does not contract with a better polytope: every
rung solves the same relaxation and feeds the same certificate, and from R1 on
they contract alike. Its advantage is the cost of an LP. The ladder separates
two parts of the cost of a call of SoPlex as Ibex makes it: the interface and
its options (R1 to R3), which a careful integration of SoPlex removes, and
what the simplex of SoPlex itself does at every LP, which only a simplex
dedicated to the pass can omit. SC pivots more than SoPlex, and each LP still costs it less.
Against the calls of the \texttt{SoPlex} class, the emulation of
Section~\ref{sec:bank} attributes most of the gap to the refactorization and
the verification that SC omits. Against \texttt{SPxSolver} driven directly,
removing those two from SoPlex recovers little: what SC saves is the
preparation of each LP as a new problem, which a solver built for one LP
repeats at every call. That SC needs no phase~1 when the objective or the box
changes, and keeps one basis for the whole pass, is what lets it skip that
preparation: the certificate makes any basis usable, so between two LPs only what the new
objective changes has to be recomputed. On small relaxations, where an LP needs two or three
pivots, that saves 20--25\% of a pass of the bare simplex.

\paragraph{Where it loses.} The saving is a fixed cost per LP, so its weight
falls as the pivots per LP grow, and beyond 30 rows they dominate. There the
cost of a pivot decides, and SoPlex has an option that SC lacks: when the rows
outnumber the variables, as with \texttt{compo}, it represents the basis by
rows, with the dimension of the variables instead of that of the rows.
Against SoPlex in the column representation SC is within 4\%
(Section~\ref{sec:bank}); the rest comes from a few degenerate LPs on which
its primal steps stall, and from systems with band structure on which it
pivots more. The homogeneous form with one slack per row, which makes the
certificate and the warm starts simple, is what ties SC to the column
representation; a self-certifying simplex in the row representation would
keep the certificate, which needs only the multipliers, and is the natural
next step. Meanwhile the hybrid uses each engine where it is better; on
systems of equations, whose best relaxation is \texttt{compo}, this is what
makes the redesigned contractor pay. On small relaxations one difference
remains unexplained: SoPlex ends 13--40\% of its LPs on optimization problems
at a vertex that is already optimal for the next bound, and needs fewer
pivots than SC (1.8 against 2.8 per LP); neither a tie-break toward the
corners of the box inside the optimal face nor a short walk inside that face
made SC end at such vertices.

\paragraph{What did not pay.} The refinements of the dual method left out of
SC (Section~\ref{sec:iter}), devex pricing and the long-step ratio test, help
on some relaxations and hurt on others: on the bank they make a pass 1.3--1.5
times faster on three of the band systems with the affine relaxation, and up to
2.1 times slower on the same systems with \texttt{compo}, and on average they leave the time of the search unchanged
(Section~\ref{sec:main}). Several ideas that use the certificate or the
tableau beyond the pass did not pay on the whole benchmark either. Certificates of an
ancestor box remain valid (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}) but rarely prune what
the node does not prune anyway. Truncating each LP when the certified bound of its current basis
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}) is close to what the LP can still gain saves
pivots ($\times0.84$) but weakens the contraction (nodes
$\times1.39$--$1.60$). Propagating with HC4 in the middle of a pass, cuts at
the LP optima, and a Lagrangian bound improved by monotonicity reduce the
nodes or tighten bounds, but cost more than they save. Inheriting the basis of
the parent node cuts the pivots of the first LP of a node by a factor of three
or more, but the first LP is a small part of the node. Earlier versions of SC
kept the whole tableau explicitly and perturbed the zero reduced costs of the
highly dual-degenerate bound problems~\cite{koberstein2005dual}; with the
other components in place both made SC slower. Doing more work inside a node
did not change the search tree; what changed it was what is computed: the
stability filter of the ratio test, the Farkas ray of the infeasible row, the
tolerance.

\paragraph{Limits.} SC removes the external LP solver from the contractor, not
from the optimizer: the upper-bounding of IbexOpt still solves its LPs with
SoPlex. On problems with very narrow equalities the upper-bounding becomes the
bottleneck: on bearing, whose eight equalities are relaxed to
$|h|\le10^{-8}$, feasible points are found only in boxes of width $10^{-4}$ to
$10^{-3}$, and the search tree changes by two orders of magnitude between
seeds; this is why bearing, alkyl and alkylbis are left out of the means of
the solver. Relaxations of
several hundred rows are still expensive for every engine: the systems of 400
to 1\,000 variables of the benchmark exceed the limits of 60\,s and 6\,GB with
all of them. And the gain of SC on the whole system solver is small, because
the pass is a small part of its nodes (5\% of the CPU in the median). One use of the certificate is left
open: when the warm-started simplex declares an infeasibility that the ray
does not certify, SC solves the LP again from the slack basis
(Section~\ref{sec:iter}), but the multipliers of the basis where it stopped,
moved along the ray as long as the bound improves, already give a certified
bound without that second LP.

\section{Conclusion}

A polytope-hull contractor solves $2n$ small, closely related LPs per node and
certifies each bound with the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound, which is valid for
any multipliers. A general-purpose LP solver cannot take advantage of this: it
treats each LP as a new problem. A simplex dedicated to the pass solves the
$2n$ problems in sequence on one basis, updating only what changes from one
LP to the next; the certificate makes it simple, since it never verifies or
refactorizes before an optimum and needs no phase~1. Switching off the
refactorization and the checks in SoPlex recovers little of the difference:
the gain is in not preparing each LP as a new problem. Measured as a ladder
inside the same contractor, from the same boxes and relaxations, a pass of
this simplex costs 0.39--0.42 times that of SoPlex as Ibex uses it on
relaxations of up to 30 rows, and 20--25\% less than the simplex of SoPlex
driven directly, with the same contraction.
Inside the search, the optimizer takes 21--22\% less CPU time than with SoPlex
as Ibex uses it, and 5--8\% less than with the bare simplex. On
larger relaxations the pivots dominate, and when the rows outnumber the
variables SoPlex makes them cheaper by representing the basis by rows:
against SoPlex in the column representation, the one of SC, a pass of SC is
within 4\%. A hybrid that chooses the engine by the size of the relaxation
keeps the best of both. A self-certifying simplex in the row representation
is the natural next step.
