\begin{abstract}
Interval branch-and-bound solvers compute rigorous enclosures of the solutions
of nonlinear systems and of global optima. Their polytope-hull contractor
bounds each of the $n$ variables of a node over a linear relaxation by solving
$2n$ small linear programs (LPs), usually with a floating-point LP solver whose
dual solution is certified afterwards. On such LPs a general-purpose solver
spends more per call on setting up, refactorizing and verifying than on
pivoting. We present a self-certifying simplex (SC) designed around the
certificate: with the relaxation in a homogeneous form, any basis yields a
valid bound on the box and its sub-boxes, so SC verifies nothing, does not
refactorize before declaring an optimum, needs no phase to find a feasible
basis, can stop an LP at any basis, and solves the $2n$ LPs on one shared
basis. In the open-source interval solver Ibex, on the same LPs and with the
same contraction, a pass of SC costs 0.55--0.62 times that of SoPlex, 0.73--0.82
times that of the simplex of SoPlex driven directly, and 0.12--0.27 times that
of HiGHS and Gurobi; with an affine relaxation, the whole solver is 16--17\%
faster on 240 global
optimization problems and 8--9\% faster on 242 systems of equations, with the
same search trees. The gain belongs to relaxations of up to 15--30 rows; a
hybrid that hands larger ones to SoPlex stays within 2\% of the better engine
everywhere.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Interval branch-and-bound solvers compute, with mathematical certainty, an
enclosure of all solutions of a system of nonlinear equations and inequalities,
or the global optimum of a constrained problem~\cite{neumaier2004complete}.
Their efficiency depends on \emph{contractors}, operators that shrink the
current box without losing solutions~\cite{chabert2009contractor}. Among them,
contractors based on linear relaxations are among the most effective: the
constraints are linearized over the box, by convex interval
Taylor forms~\cite{araya2012xnewton} or affine
arithmetic~\cite{ninin2015affine,araya2025hybridizing}, and each variable is
minimized and maximized over the resulting polytope. In Ibex this is the
polytope-hull contractor, the linear part of X-Newton, which we introduced
in~\cite{araya2012xnewton}; we later combined the affine and Taylor-based
relaxations~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}.

Each call solves up to $2n$ linear programs, and the rigor of the result cannot
come from the LP solver, which works in floating point. Current implementations
therefore treat the LP solver as a black box and certify its output a
posteriori: the dual solution is turned into a safe bound by the
Neumaier--Shcherbina procedure~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, and an infeasibility
claim is accepted only if the returned Farkas ray passes the same verification.
This design has a cost that is easy to miss: the $2n$ LPs of a node are small
and closely related, typically two or three pivots apart, and each is a
separate call to a general-purpose solver, which sets up its vectors,
refactorizes the basis before declaring an optimum and verifies its solution;
on these LPs that work exceeds the pivots themselves
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

We take the opposite approach and design the simplex around the certificate.
With the relaxation written in a homogeneous form, with one interval-bounded
slack per row, \emph{any} multiplier vector yields a valid bound on the box and
on every sub-box, so the simplex never needs to be correct: it verifies
nothing, and it does not refactorize before declaring an optimum. Every basis
can also be made dual feasible by relocating its nonbasic variables, so each of
the $2n$ bound problems, and each problem after the box contracts, starts dual
feasible without a phase~1 (the search for a feasible starting basis); the
$2n$ problems share one basis; and with the dual method the current basis
certifies a bound at every iteration, so an LP can be stopped at any time
without losing it. We call this simplex \emph{self-certifying} (SC).

\paragraph{Positioning.} The certificate itself is not new. That any
multiplier vector yields a valid bound once the residual is evaluated in
interval arithmetic is the principle of Neumaier and
Shcherbina~\cite{neumaier2004safe}. Domes and Neumaier~\cite{domes2012rigorous}
applied it to filtering with linear relaxations: they solve the bound LPs with
an external solver, collect the multipliers, and solve the preconditioned
interval system row by row. In previous work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning} we
wrote the relaxation in the homogeneous form used here and showed that the
optimal preconditioner for an interval Gauss--Seidel projection is given by the
dual solutions of the bound LPs; the equivalent valid inequalities appear in
the optimization-based bound tightening of Gleixner et
al.~\cite{gleixner2017three}, and both works reuse them in sub-boxes. In all
these approaches the multipliers are a by-product of an external solver and
certification is a post-processing step, whose cost leads to applying it to a
few promising variables~\cite{domes2012rigorous}, or to recomputing it only
periodically and reusing it in between~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}. What
changes with SC is \emph{where the multipliers come from and how often a usable
certificate is available}: the simplex itself produces them, at every basis.
On the solver side, Yamamura and
coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} observed that, after a
bisection, the optimal tableau of the parent remains dual feasible for the
child, so that an LP emptiness test can be resumed by the dual simplex method
with few or no pivots per box; their warm start concerns a single feasibility
LP on a fixed relaxation, without rigorous certification, and SC extends it to
the $2n$ objectives of the bound problems. Bound tightening by optimization is
also central in MINLP solvers~\cite{belotti2009branching,gleixner2017three},
where rigor is not required; the heuristic of skipping bounds already attained
by a primal point, used by Ibex, comes from Baharev, Achterberg and
R\'ev~\cite{baharev2009distillation}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A design principle.} We show that the certificate, usually applied
after the LP solver has finished, can instead shape the simplex method that
computes the multipliers: since every basis certifies a bound and can be made
dual feasible for free~\cite{koberstein2005dual}, the work that a
general-purpose solver spends on making its answer correct is no longer
needed (Sections~\ref{sec:simplex} and~\ref{sec:properties}).
\item \emph{A simplex built on it.} We describe the components that make such
a simplex competitive on the $2n$ bound problems of a node, from the order in
which the bounds are solved and the way each LP starts from the previous one
to the merging of redundant rows and the pivoting rules, and we measure what
each of them contributes (Section~\ref{sec:simplex}).
\item \emph{Properties of the certificate.} We prove that it is valid for any
multipliers and on every sub-box, that it is exact at an optimal basis, and
that under the dual method it gives a valid bound that never decreases along
the iterations (Section~\ref{sec:properties}).
\item \emph{A controlled evaluation.} We compare SC with SoPlex, HiGHS and
Gurobi inside the same contractor of Ibex, on the same LPs and with the same
certificate, so that only the engine changes. SC makes a pass of the
contractor markedly cheaper with the same contraction, and the whole solver
faster on both systems of equations and optimization problems. The analysis
also shows where the approach stops paying, on large relaxations, explains
why, and suggests a hybrid contractor that uses each engine where it is
better (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Interval solvers and linear relaxations.}
Interval branch and bound alternates bisection with contraction. Ibex, on
which we build, composes constraint propagation (HC4~\cite{benhamou1999revising}),
shaving-based consistencies such as ACID~\cite{neveu2015adaptive}, the interval
Newton operator for square systems~\cite{neumaier1990interval}, and contractors
based on linear relaxations~\cite{chabert2009contractor}. The polytope-hull
contractor computes a linear relaxation of the constraints over the box and
bounds each variable over it; in X-Newton~\cite{araya2012xnewton} the
relaxation comes from convex interval Taylor forms, and the affine arithmetic
relaxation of Ninin et al.~\cite{ninin2015affine} is an alternative which we
later combined with it~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}. QUAD~\cite{lebbah2005quad}
and GloptLab~\cite{domes2012rigorous} use linear relaxations in the same way.
In previous work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning} we wrote the relaxation in the
homogeneous form used here and showed that the optimal preconditioner for a
Gauss--Seidel projection over it is given by the dual solutions of the bound
LPs. Yamamura and coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} solve an LP
emptiness test at each box of a bisection search and resume it by the dual
simplex method from the parent's tableau.

\paragraph{Rigorous bounds for linear programs.}
A floating-point LP solver returns approximate solutions, and two lines of
work make its results rigorous. The first certifies them a posteriori.
Neumaier and Shcherbina~\cite{neumaier2004safe} showed that any approximate
dual vector yields a safe bound once the residual is evaluated in interval
arithmetic, and that an approximate Farkas ray proves infeasibility under the
same verification; Jansson~\cite{jansson2004rigorous} gave rigorous lower and
upper bounds based on the same principle, implemented in
Lurupa~\cite{keil2006computational}. In filtering, Domes and
Neumaier~\cite{domes2012rigorous} apply this certificate to the bound LPs of a
linear relaxation, collecting the multipliers from an external solver for a
few promising variables. The second line solves the LP exactly, in rational
arithmetic~\cite{applegate2007exact}, or to arbitrary precision by iterative
refinement~\cite{gleixner2016iterative}, as needed in exact mixed-integer
programming~\cite{cook2013hybrid}. Both treat the LP solver as given. The
present work keeps the a posteriori certificate, which costs no more than one
matrix--vector product, and changes the solver instead.

\paragraph{Optimization-based bound tightening.}
Minimizing and maximizing each variable over a relaxation is also a standard
bound tightening technique in global optimization, where rigor is not
required: range reduction in BARON~\cite{ryoo1996branch}, and
optimization-based bound tightening in Couenne~\cite{belotti2009branching} and
SCIP. Gleixner et al.~\cite{gleixner2017three} make it cheaper by filtering
bounds already attained by feasible points, by ordering the LPs greedily, and
by turning the dual solutions into valid inequalities that are propagated in
the subtree. The skipping rule used by Ibex comes from Baharev, Achterberg and
R\'ev~\cite{baharev2009distillation}: a bound attained by a primal point found
earlier needs no LP, and the next LP is warm-started from the previous one.

\paragraph{Simplex techniques.}
Our simplex uses standard ingredients of modern implementations, described in
detail by Koberstein~\cite{koberstein2005dual} for the dual method: the
bounded-variable dual simplex with the long-step (bound flipping) ratio
test~\cite{maros2003generalized}, Harris' ratio test and devex
pricing~\cite{harris1973devex}, an approximation of steepest
edge~\cite{forrest1992steepest}, and the revised method with a sparse LU
factorization of the basis updated by Forrest and Tomlin's
method~\cite{forrest1972updated}, as in SoPlex~\cite{wunderling1996paralleler}.
What is specific to our setting is not any of these techniques but their use
for $2n$ closely related LPs over the same constraints, with a certificate
attached to every basis.

\section{The homogeneous relaxation and its certificate}\label{sec:dfb}

Let $\iv{x}\in\IR^n$ be the current box. A linearizer returns a matrix
$A\in\R^{m\times n}$ of floating-point numbers and an interval vector
$\iv{b}\in\IR^m$, possibly with infinite bounds, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relax}
x \in \iv{x} \text{ is a solution} \;\Longrightarrow\; A x \in \iv{b}.
\end{equation}
We introduce one slack per row, $s = Ax$, and write the relaxation in the
homogeneous form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:homog}
\Abar z = 0,\qquad z = (x,s)\in \iv{z} := \iv{x}\times\iv{s},\qquad
\Abar := [\,A \;\; -I_m\,],
\end{equation}
where $\iv{s}_i := \iv{b}_i \cap \square(A_{i:}\,\iv{x})$ and
$\square(\cdot)$ denotes evaluation in interval arithmetic. We call
$P(\iv{x}) := \{ z\in\iv{z} : \Abar z = 0\}$ the \emph{relaxation polytope}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:relax}
If $x^*\in\iv{x}$ is a solution, then $(x^*, Ax^*)\in P(\iv{x})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$A_{i:}x^*\in\iv{b}_i$ by~\eqref{eq:relax} and $A_{i:}x^*\in\square(A_{i:}\iv{x})$
by the inclusion property of interval arithmetic; the equation holds by
construction of $s$.
\end{proof}

The certificate combines the $m$ rows of $\Abar z=0$ linearly, with one
\emph{multiplier} per row, into a single equation $\gamma^Tz=0$ that every point
of the polytope satisfies. Any vector of multipliers $\lambda\in\R^m$ gives a
valid equation; the best ones for bounding $z_k$ are the dual solutions of the
LP that minimizes or maximizes $z_k$ over the polytope
(Section~\ref{sec:properties}), and the simplex of Section~\ref{sec:simplex}
produces them.

\begin{definition}[Certificate]\label{def:cert}
For a vector of multipliers $\lambda\in\R^m$, let $\iv{\gamma}\in\IR^{n+m}$ be an interval vector that
contains $\gamma := \Abar^T\lambda$, computed in outward-rounded interval
arithmetic. For $k\in\{1,\dots,n+m\}$ with $0\notin\iv{\gamma}_k$, the
\emph{certified enclosure} of $z_k$ is
\[
\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z}) := -\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j\neq k}\iv{\gamma}_j\,\iv{z}_j\Big) \Big/ \iv{\gamma}_k ,
\]
and the \emph{residual} is $\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z}) := \sum_{j}\iv{\gamma}_j\,\iv{z}_j$.
\end{definition}

The idea is a single line. Every point $z$ of the polytope satisfies
$\Abar z=0$, hence $\gamma^T z=\lambda^T\Abar z=0$ for any $\lambda$, that is,
$\gamma_k z_k=-\sum_{j\neq k}\gamma_j z_j$. Bounding the right-hand side over
the box in interval arithmetic, and dividing by $\gamma_k$, encloses $z_k$; and
if the residual $\iv{R}$ excludes zero, no point of the box can satisfy
$\gamma^Tz=0$, so the polytope is empty. A contraction step therefore replaces
$\iv{z}_k$ by $\iv{z}_k\cap\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$, and an emptiness test
checks $0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$. Section~\ref{sec:properties} states
this formally (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}) and shows when the enclosure is
tight; Section~\ref{sec:simplex} explains how the simplex produces $\lambda$.

\paragraph{Why not a rigorous simplex.}
Validity does not depend on how $\lambda$ was computed: any vector of
multipliers gives a valid bound, and a poor one only gives a weaker bound. Rigor is
therefore needed only in the final evaluation of $\iv{\gamma}$ and
$\iv{C}_k$, not in the pivoting. Carrying intervals through the pivots
instead pays the overestimation of interval Gaussian
elimination~\cite{neumaier1990interval}, whose widths grow with the number of
pivots, and solving each LP exactly in rational arithmetic, or to high
precision by iterative refinement~\cite{applegate2007exact,gleixner2016iterative},
buys an exactness that the bound does not need, at a cost that $2n$ LPs per
node cannot afford. An early version of our contractor pivoted in interval
arithmetic: on a one-box benchmark it had to rebuild the tableau from the
original relaxation 731 times to control the width growth, and pivoting in
floating point with certification of the final multipliers only was 2.8 times
faster with essentially the same contraction (mean 24.0\% against 24.6\%).

\section{A simplex for the \texorpdfstring{$2n$}{2n} bound problems}\label{sec:simplex}

For each structural variable $x_k$ the contractor needs the two bounds of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpk}
\mathrm{LP}_k:\quad \min\; z_k \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Abar z = 0,\; z\in\iv{z},
\end{equation}
and of its maximization, obtained with $-z_k$. A basis is a set of $m$ columns
of $\Abar$ forming a nonsingular matrix $B$; with cost vector $c=\pm e_k$, the
multipliers are $\lambda^T = c_B^T B^{-1}$ and the reduced costs
$d = c - \Abar^T\lambda$. For the bounded-variable simplex, a basis with each
nonbasic variable at one of its bounds is \emph{dual feasible} if $d_j\ge 0$ for
nonbasic variables at their lower bound and $d_j\le 0$ for those at their upper
bound, and \emph{primal feasible} if the basic variables lie within their
bounds. This section describes how the simplex solves the $2n$ problems of a
node; the design choices are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{What the certificate makes unnecessary.} Since the certificate of
Section~\ref{sec:dfb} is valid for any $\lambda$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}),
the simplex never has to return a correct solution, only a good basis. A
general-purpose LP solver, before declaring an optimum, refactorizes the basis
to remove the error accumulated by the updates of its factorization,
recomputes its vectors and verifies the solution, and the contractor then
verifies it once more through the certificate. SC uses the final basis as it
is: an inaccurate $\lambda$ yields a weaker bound, never an invalid one, so the
error of the factorization is controlled only as far as pivoting requires.
Likewise, since every basis can be made dual feasible
(Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), no phase~1 is needed when the objective changes
or the box contracts, and the $2n$ problems can share one basis.
Section~\ref{sec:bank} measures what these savings are worth against SoPlex.

The rest of this section describes the simplex that results:
how one pass solves the bounds of a node and in which order
(Section~\ref{sec:pass}), how each LP starts from the previous one
(Section~\ref{sec:warm}), the iterations (Section~\ref{sec:iter}), the
factorization of the basis (Section~\ref{sec:engines}), and how the
certificate is computed when an LP ends (Section~\ref{sec:certif}).

\subsection{One pass over the bounds}\label{sec:pass}

At each node the relaxation is computed, written in the
form~\eqref{eq:homog}, and loaded once. Rows that are proportional, within a
relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, are merged into one row whose slack interval is
the intersection of their scaled intervals; the polytope does not change. The
affine linearizer writes each equation as two one-sided rows with the same
normal, so merging halves $m$ on square systems, and the combined relaxation
of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} writes some rows twice, with opposite signs,
far apart in the matrix. The contractor then makes one pass over the up to
$2n$ bounds with a single simplex: each LP starts from the final basis of the
previous one. When an LP ends, its bound is certified once, from the final
basis, and the box is contracted before the next LP starts. Since the bound of
an optimal LP is attained by the polytope, this does not change the optima of
the following LPs; it only tightens the certificates of LPs that stop before
their optimum (below).

Two rules decide which LPs are solved and in which order. A bound is
\emph{skipped} when a primal point obtained earlier in the pass already
attains it, since its LP would return that value; this rule comes from the
polytope-hull contractor of Ibex~\cite{baharev2009distillation}. Among the
remaining bounds, the contractor of Ibex solves first the one farthest from the
last primal point. SC solves first the nearest one: its optimum lies closest to
the vertex where the previous LP ended, so the next LP needs fewer pivots. In
the experiments of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, the external LP solvers we
compare with SC use the same order, which is also the best one for them.

If the residual $\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$ of an LP excludes zero, the node is
empty and the pass stops. If an LP ends without an optimal basis, its bound is
still certified with the multipliers of the last basis, and the pass goes on.

\paragraph{A limit on the pivots of each LP.} Since any basis certifies, an LP
can be stopped at any basis with a valid, possibly weaker, bound. SC stops each
LP after 60 pivots. With the affine relaxation the limit is rarely reached and
changes nothing; with larger relaxations it cuts the long chains of degenerate
pivots of a few LPs (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). With an external LP
solver the same would be possible, since the multipliers of the basis where it
stops would also certify a bound, but the usual interface does not use them:
SoPlex stores a solution when it reaches its iteration limit, yet Ibex treats
the LP as unsolved, discards its bound and abandons the pass. By default Ibex
limits SoPlex to 100 iterations per LP, and on Geneig-icse 377 of 61\,815 LPs
reach that limit. In our experiments the external solvers keep the policy of
Ibex, but we raise their limit from 100 to $20m+200$ iterations, where $m$ is
the number of rows, so that they almost never reach it (8 LPs of 68\,757 on
the same system).

\subsection{Starting and warm-starting}\label{sec:warm}

The first LP of a pass has no previous basis. It starts from the slack basis
$B=-I_m$, which is dual feasible for every $\mathrm{LP}_k$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), and is solved by the dual method, with no
phase~1.

Between two consecutive LPs of the pass the objective changes, and the
contracted bound changes the box. Two warm starts are possible from the
previous optimal basis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{dual}: relocate every nonbasic variable to the bound indicated by
the sign of its new reduced cost, $z_k$ included, which restores dual
feasibility (Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), and continue with the dual method;
the certified bound is then available, and nondecreasing, at every iteration
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mono});
\item \emph{primal}: keep the nonbasic variables where they are; the basis
remains primal feasible, up to the contracted bound, and is reoptimized with
primal steps, which is the usual warm start of an LP solver.
\end{itemize}
Both reach an optimal basis, so the certificate at termination is exact
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}) and the pruning is the same; whether the node is
empty is decided in the first LP, common to both. They differ only in cost.

The cost depends on how far apart the optima of consecutive bounds are. An
optimal vertex is given by a basis and by the bound on which each nonbasic
variable rests, and a nonbasic variable that must move to its opposite bound
costs the two warm starts differently: the primal one pays an iteration for it,
the dual one moves it for free, since relocation is not a pivot. Relocation has
its own cost, however, paid once per LP: it changes the values of the nonbasic
variables, and the basic values must be recomputed from the factorization.
So the primal warm start is cheaper when few variables change side, and the
dual one when many do.

How many change side depends on the rows. On systems of equations every row
comes from an equation and is a narrow slab: the interval of its slack is, on
average, 13\% of the range of the row over the box, so the optima of
consecutive bounds often rest on opposite faces of a slab. On optimization
problems the slack intervals cover 48\% of the range, and few rows change side.
Table~\ref{tab:sides} measures it over the benchmarks of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. The basis changes by 10--17\% in both programs,
but a median of 1.9 nonbasic variables per LP change side on systems of
equations, against 0.3 on optimization problems. The iterations follow: on
systems the primal warm start makes 5.1 per LP against 3.1 for the dual one,
about one more per side change; on optimization problems it makes 2.1
against 2.5.

In time, the two effects balance differently in each program. On
optimization problems the primal warm start pivots as little as the dual one
and saves the relocation: in the whole solver, the dual warm start is 4\%
slower (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). On systems of equations the two extra
primal pivots per LP cost about as much as the relocation they avoid, and both
warm starts take the same time. SC therefore uses the primal warm start in the
optimizer, where the first LP of each pass is still solved by the dual method,
and the dual warm start in the solver of systems of equations. There the two
cost the same and the choice matters little; we keep the dual one so that the
whole pass runs the dual method of its first LP, with primal steps only as a
fallback. With the larger relaxation of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} neither warm start wins on every system, and SC
chooses per run: it alternates the two on the first nodes, keeps the one with
the lower cost per LP, measured as a moving average, and tries the other again
every 25 nodes. With the primal warm start, primal steps are also the fallback
when relocation is impossible because the bound indicated by a reduced cost is
infinite; with the dual one, SC takes no primal steps
(Section~\ref{sec:properties}).

\subsection{Simplex iterations}\label{sec:iter}

We recall the bounded-variable simplex in the notation of
$\mathrm{LP}_k$~\eqref{eq:lpk}, following~\cite{koberstein2005dual}. Let $B$ be
the basis matrix, $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ the basic and nonbasic
index sets, and $x_N$ the nonbasic variables, each at one of its bounds; the
basic values are $x_B = -B^{-1}\Abar_{\mathcal{N}}x_N$, since the right-hand
side of~\eqref{eq:homog} is zero. Every iteration exchanges one basic and one
nonbasic variable and needs three vectors:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{pivot column} $\alpha_q = B^{-1}\Abar_{:q}$ of the entering
variable $z_q$, obtained by solving $B\alpha_q = \Abar_{:q}$ (forward
transformation, FTRAN);
\item the \emph{pivot row} $\alpha_r^T = e_r^T B^{-1}\Abar$ of the variable
leaving at position $r$, obtained by solving $B^T\rho_r = e_r$ (backward
transformation, BTRAN) and then computing $\alpha_r^T = \rho_r^T\Abar$;
\item the step lengths: a primal step $\theta_p$ for $x_B$ and a dual step
$\theta_d = d_q/\alpha_{rq}$ for $(\lambda,d)$.
\end{itemize}
The updates are then
\[
x_B \leftarrow x_B - \theta_p\,\alpha_q,\qquad
\lambda \leftarrow \lambda + \theta_d\,\rho_r,\qquad
d \leftarrow d - \theta_d\,\alpha_r ,
\]
with $z_q$ taking position $r$ of the basis. The certificate needs $\lambda$,
which is therefore always at hand.

\paragraph{Dual steps.} In a dual iteration the basis is dual feasible and
some basic variable violates its bounds. The leaving variable is chosen among
the violated basic variables by the largest ratio of the squared violation to
a \emph{devex} weight~\cite{harris1973devex}, a cheap approximation of the
norm of the corresponding row of $B^{-1}$ that avoids the bias of the plain
largest-violation rule towards badly scaled rows. The entering variable comes
from the ratio test over the pivot row: among the nonbasic $z_j$ whose reduced
cost would change sign in the wrong direction, the one with the smallest
$|d_j/\alpha_{rj}|$ keeps the basis dual feasible. We use the long-step (bound
flipping) version of this test~\cite{maros2003generalized,koberstein2005dual}:
the dual objective along the ray is piecewise linear and concave, with a
breakpoint at each ratio; when a breakpoint belongs to a boxed variable, the
step can go past it by moving that variable to its other bound, and the test
continues while the slope remains positive. One iteration thus does the work
of several short steps. Among the candidates at the chosen breakpoint we take
the largest $|\alpha_{rj}|$ within a relative band (Harris' rule), which
avoids tiny pivots.

\paragraph{Primal steps.} In a primal iteration the basis is primal feasible
and some nonbasic variable has a reduced cost of the wrong sign; it enters
(Dantzig's rule: largest $|d_j|$), and the ratio test over the pivot column
$\alpha_q$ finds the first basic variable to reach a bound, or lets $z_q$ move
to its own opposite bound. Pivots below a stability threshold are filtered
out before choosing the leaving variable; without this filter a rejected
pivot was indistinguishable from optimality, and 40\% of the reported optima
were not dual feasible. SC takes primal steps only with the primal warm start:
to reoptimize after each bound, and to repair the nonbasic variables that
could not be relocated.

\paragraph{Tolerances and infinite bounds.} The primal and dual feasibility
tolerances are $10^{-9}$, relative to the magnitude of the value tested; with
the combined relaxation of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, whose nearly parallel
rows make the LPs degenerate, we use $10^{-6}$ in the solver of systems of
equations. A tolerance is a matter of efficiency, not of rigor. A tolerance too
wide accepts an empty polytope as feasible, which only delays the pruning, and
weakens the contraction: with $10^{-6}$ and the affine relaxation, SC explores
twice the cells on Geneig-icse. A tolerance too tight makes the warm-started
simplex report infeasibility on boxes that are not empty; the ray then fails
the residual test, and SC solves that LP again from the slack basis instead of
discarding it. Slacks may have an
infinite side; a nonbasic variable always sits at a finite bound, the other
one if the bound indicated by its reduced cost is infinite, and at zero if it
is free.

\subsection{The engine}\label{sec:engines}

SC keeps a sparse LU factorization $B = LU$, computed by the factorization
code of SoPlex, and obtains each iteration's vectors by triangular solves:
FTRAN solves $Ly=\Abar_{:q}$ and then $U\alpha_q = y$, BTRAN solves $U^Tw=e_r$
and then $L^T\rho_r=w$. Since the basis changes by one column per iteration,
the factors are not recomputed but updated: in the Forrest--Tomlin
update~\cite{forrest1972updated} the new column replaces column $r$ of $U$,
which breaks its triangular shape; a permutation moves it to the end, and one
row elimination, stored as a sparse row transformation appended to $L$,
restores the triangle. The update reuses the vector $y$ of the last FTRAN, so
it costs about one more solve. Updates make the factors grow, and SC
refactorizes $B$ from scratch every 50 pivots. The FTRAN of the entering
column is computed once per iteration and serves three purposes: the devex
weights, the Forrest--Tomlin update and the update of $x_B$, which is
recomputed from scratch every 32 pivots to limit drift. The reduced costs and
$\lambda$ are updated with the pivot row as above. An iteration costs one
FTRAN, one BTRAN, one product $\rho_r^T\Abar$ and the update, all
proportional to the number of nonzeros involved.

\subsection{Certification}\label{sec:certif}

When an LP terminates, $\lambda$ is read from the final basis, where it is
kept updated with the pivot row. The certificate $\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$ is computed
once per bound in outward-rounded interval arithmetic, over the rows with
$\lambda_i\neq 0$, and $\iv{C}_k$ and $\iv{R}$ follow in $O(n+m)$
(Definition~\ref{def:cert}). When a dual iteration finds a basic variable out
of its bounds and no entering variable in its row $r$, the polytope is empty,
and the row $r$ of $B^{-1}$ is a Farkas ray: SC takes $\lambda=\rho_r$, and the
residual test turns it into a proof of emptiness. The simplex itself never needs to be correct:
a wrong pivot or an inaccurate $\lambda$ yields a weaker bound, never an
invalid one (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}).

\section{Properties of the certificate}\label{sec:properties}

All statements hold for the floating-point $A$ returned by the linearizer;
rounding in the computation of $\iv{\gamma}$ and $\iv{C}_k$ is covered by
outward rounding. We write $e_k$ for the $k$-th unit vector of $\R^{n+m}$.

\subsection{Validity}

\begin{proposition}[Validity for any multipliers]\label{prop:valid}
Let $\lambda\in\R^m$ be arbitrary. For every $z\in P(\iv{x})$ and every $k$ with
$0\notin\iv{\gamma}_k$, we have $z_k\in\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$. If
$0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$, then $P(\iv{x})=\emptyset$, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:relax} the box $\iv{x}$ contains no solution.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\gamma=\Abar^T\lambda$ be the exact vector, so $\gamma\in\iv{\gamma}$. For
$z\in P(\iv{x})$, $\gamma^T z = \lambda^T\Abar z = 0$, hence
$\gamma_k z_k = -\sum_{j\ne k}\gamma_j z_j$. Each product $\gamma_j z_j$ belongs
to $\iv{\gamma}_j\iv{z}_j$ and the sum to the interval sum; dividing by
$\gamma_k\in\iv{\gamma}_k$, which does not contain $0$, gives
$z_k\in\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$ by the inclusion property. For the second
claim, any $z\in P(\iv{x})$ would give $0=\gamma^T z\in\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$.
\end{proof}

No property of $\lambda$ is used: it need not be optimal, feasible, or
accurate. Errors in $\lambda$ can only weaken the enclosure, never invalidate
it. In particular, a Farkas multiplier read from the simplex when it detects
primal infeasibility either passes the residual test or is simply not used:
there is no ``infeasible but unproved'' outcome that forces the pass to stop.
A warm-started simplex can, however, accumulate enough error to report
infeasibility on boxes that are not empty: the ray then fails the test, the
bound is merely not contracted, and the node is bisected instead of pruned.
When this happens SC solves the same LP again from a cold start. In the
benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} this retry changes nothing at
60\,s (the search is identical in 96\% of the runs), but it removes the rare
long stalls in which the warm start reports such infeasibility over a whole
region of the tree (Section~\ref{sec:long}).

\begin{proposition}[Validity on sub-boxes]\label{prop:subbox}
Let $\iv{x}'\subseteq\iv{x}$ and let $\iv{z}'\subseteq\iv{z}$ be the
corresponding box of~\eqref{eq:homog}, built with the matrix $\Abar$ computed
on $\iv{x}$. Then every solution in $\iv{x}'$ satisfies
$z_k\in\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z}')\subseteq\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The linearization~\eqref{eq:relax} on $\iv{x}$ remains valid for solutions in
$\iv{x}'$, so $P'=\{z\in\iv{z}':\Abar z=0\}\subseteq P(\iv{x})$ contains them,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:valid} applies to $P'$. The inclusion follows from
the inclusion isotonicity of interval arithmetic.
\end{proof}

A certificate computed at a node can thus be reused in all its descendants,
as we did with the preconditioner of~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}
and Gleixner et al.\ with their dual-derived
inequalities~\cite{gleixner2017three}. It stays valid, but it was optimized for
the parent box and is usually weak in a child; with SC, where a fresh
certificate costs one LP, we measured such reuse and it does not pay
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Tightness}

Consider the bound problem $\mathrm{LP}_k$~\eqref{eq:lpk} with $\iv{z}$
bounded, and the notation of Section~\ref{sec:simplex}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:dualfun}
For any $\lambda$ with $d = e_k - \Abar^T\lambda$ and $d_k=0$, the exact
certificate satisfies $\gamma_k=1$ and
\[
\inff \iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z}) \;=\; \sum_{j\neq k}\min_{z_j\in\iv{z}_j} d_j z_j
\;=\; \min_{z\in\iv{z}} d^T z \;=: g(\lambda),
\]
the value of the Lagrangian dual function of~\eqref{eq:lpk} at $\lambda$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\gamma=\Abar^T\lambda = e_k - d$, so $\gamma_k = 1-d_k = 1$ and $\gamma_j=-d_j$
for $j\ne k$. Then
$\iv{C}_k = \sum_{j\ne k} d_j\iv{z}_j$, whose infimum is the sum of the minima of
the independent terms. The term $j=k$ of $d^Tz$ vanishes. Finally, the
Lagrangian of~\eqref{eq:lpk} relaxing $\Abar z=0$ with multiplier $\lambda$ is
$z_k - \lambda^T\Abar z = d^T z$, minimized over the box $\iv{z}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Exactness at an optimal basis]\label{prop:exact}
Let $B$ be an optimal basis of~\eqref{eq:lpk} with multipliers $\lambda^*$.
If $z_k$ is basic, then in exact arithmetic
$\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda^*,\iv{z})$ equals the optimal value of $\mathrm{LP}_k$.
If $z_k$ is nonbasic at its lower bound, the optimal value is $\inff\iv{z}_k$ and
there is nothing to contract.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $z_k$ is basic, $d_k=0$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:dualfun} gives
$\inff\iv{C}_k=g(\lambda^*)$. By weak duality $g(\lambda^*)$ is a lower bound
of the optimum. The basic solution $\bar z$ places each nonbasic variable at
the bound that minimizes $d_j z_j$ (optimality signs) and has $d_j=0$ on basic
variables, so $d^T\bar z=g(\lambda^*)$; since $\bar z\in P(\iv{x})$,
$\bar z_k = e_k^T\bar z = d^T\bar z + \lambda^{*T}\Abar\bar z = g(\lambda^*)$.
If $z_k$ is nonbasic at its lower bound, $\bar z_k=\inff\iv{z}_k$ is feasible
and optimal.
\end{proof}

The remaining case, $z_k$ nonbasic at its upper bound in an optimal basis of the
minimization, means that $P(\iv{x})$ lies in the hyperplane
$z_k=\supp\iv{z}_k$. Then $d_k\le 0$, so $\gamma_k=1-d_k\ge 1$, and the same
computation as in Lemma~\ref{lem:dualfun}, divided by $\gamma_k$, shows that the
certificate is exact as well.

In floating point, the computed enclosure exceeds the LP bound by at most
$\sum_j \wid(\iv{\gamma}_j)\,\magn(\iv{z}_j)$ plus the effect of the inaccuracy of
$\lambda$, the same slack as in the Neumaier--Shcherbina
bound~\cite{neumaier2004safe}. On identical boxes, SC attains the LP hull in
96\% of the bounds where it contracts more than the standard contractor
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\begin{remark}[Relation to Neumaier--Shcherbina and Domes--Neumaier]
Written back in the variables $x$, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$ is the
Neumaier--Shcherbina safe lower bound of $\min x_k$ subject to $Ax\in\iv{b}$,
$x\in\iv{x}$, with approximate dual $\lambda$~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, and the
row-wise solution of the preconditioned system in the linear bounding of Domes
and Neumaier~\cite[\S5]{domes2012rigorous}. In the homogeneous form,
Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} is our earlier
result~\cite[Prop.~1]{reyes2023preconditioning} that the dual solution of the
bound LP is an optimal preconditioner for the Gauss--Seidel projection, with
the vector $\lambda^T\Abar$ playing the role of a preconditioned row. The
certificate is therefore the known one; Propositions~\ref{prop:start}
and~\ref{prop:mono} are what make it cheap and always available inside the
simplex.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Dual feasibility for free}

\begin{proposition}[Dual-feasible start for every bound]\label{prop:start}
The slack basis $B=-I_m$ is dual feasible for $\mathrm{LP}_k$, for every
structural variable $k\le n$ with $\inff\iv{z}_k$ finite. More generally, if
every $\iv{z}_j$ is bounded, any basis becomes dual feasible after placing each
nonbasic $z_j$ at its lower bound if $d_j>0$ and at its upper bound if $d_j<0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With the slack basis, $c_B=0$ for $k\le n$, so $\lambda=0$ and $d=e_k$: the only
nonzero reduced cost is $d_k=1$, and $z_k$ at its lower bound satisfies the
sign condition. In general, dual feasibility is a condition on the signs of
$d_j$ for nonbasic variables relative to the bound at which they sit; $d$ does
not depend on that placement, so the relocation enforces the condition
whenever the required bound is finite.
\end{proof}

When the objective changes from one bound to the next, or the box is
contracted between two bounds, relocation restores dual feasibility without a
phase~1. That a boxed LP needs no dual phase~1 is a standard
observation~\cite{koberstein2005dual}; what we use is that it holds across the
$2n$ objectives and the contractions of the box, which is what allows one
basis to be shared by the $2n$ problems. The
case of a box change was exploited by Yamamura and
coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} to resume an LP emptiness test
after a bisection; Proposition~\ref{prop:start} extends it to a change of
objective, which is what the $2n$ bound problems need.
Relocation needs the bound indicated by the sign of the reduced cost to be
finite. Slacks of one-sided rows, and variables with an unbounded domain, may
not have it. In the implementation such a nonbasic variable is placed on its
finite bound, which leaves its reduced cost with the wrong sign, or at zero if
it is free. The iteration loop removes these dual infeasibilities with primal
steps, and uses dual steps while some basic variable is out of its bounds; with
the dual warm start SC takes no primal steps, and
an LP that ends with such a variable gives a valid, possibly weaker bound. The
certificate is unaffected, since it is valid for any $\lambda$; only the
monotonicity of Proposition~\ref{prop:mono} is lost in such an LP until the
basis is dual feasible again.

\subsection{An anytime certified bound under the dual method}

\begin{proposition}[Monotone certified bound]\label{prop:mono}
Run the dual simplex method on~\eqref{eq:lpk} from a dual-feasible basis, with
the standard or the long-step ratio test. At every iteration $t$ in which $z_k$
is basic, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda_t,\iv{z})$ is a valid lower bound on $z_k$ over
$P(\iv{x})$, and, in exact arithmetic, these bounds are nondecreasing in $t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Validity is Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}. With $z_k$ basic, $d_k=0$ and
Lemma~\ref{lem:dualfun} identifies the bound with the dual objective
$g(\lambda_t)$. Every iterate of the dual simplex is dual feasible, and each
iteration moves $\lambda$ along a dual ascent direction with a step chosen by
the ratio test; the long-step test maximizes the piecewise-linear dual function
along that direction~\cite{maros2003generalized}. Hence $g(\lambda_t)$ is
nondecreasing, strictly so on nondegenerate iterations.
\end{proof}

The certificate can thus be read at any point of the dual method, and an LP
can be truncated with a valid bound. SC uses this only as a safeguard, with a
limit of 60 pivots per LP (Section~\ref{sec:simplex}); truncating every LP
when its bound comes close to what it can still gain saves pivots but weakens
the contraction more than it saves (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). In the solver of systems of equations,
where SC uses the dual warm start (Section~\ref{sec:simplex}), the property
holds in every LP of the pass; in the optimizer it holds in the first LP of
each pass.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Certifying a bound costs the same in SC as the Neumaier--Shcherbina
post-processing. Both compute a product of the transposed constraint matrix by
the multipliers, restricted to the rows with $\lambda_i\neq 0$, plus $O(n+m)$
operations: $\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$ and $\iv{C}_k$ in SC, and the
residual $A^T\lambda-c$ and the bound $\lambda^T\iv{b}-(A^T\lambda-c)^T\iv{x}$
in Neumaier--Shcherbina. On three optimization problems (ex6\_1\_3, ex7\_2\_3,
chem) the SC certificate costs 1.3--3.1\,$\mu$s per LP, and the
post-processing 1.9--3.0\,$\mu$s plus 1.2--1.5\,$\mu$s to extract the solution
from the external solver. The difference between the two approaches is
therefore not the cost of a certificate but where $\lambda$ comes from, which is
what Section~\ref{sec:experiments} isolates.

\section{Experimental evaluation}\label{sec:experiments}


\paragraph{A controlled comparison.}
To measure the contribution of the simplex, and not of the rest of the
contractor, we compare engines inside the \emph{same} contractor. All run
the same pass over the $2n$ bounds, on the same merged relaxation, with the
same skipping rule, the same certificate and the same policy when an LP yields
no certificate: that bound is not contracted and the pass continues. (The
polytope-hull contractor of Ibex~2.8.9 abandons the pass when the LP solver
reports infeasibility without a verifiable ray or reaches its iteration limit;
our contractor continues with the next bound, whatever the engine.) They
differ only in where the multipliers come from:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{SC}: the multipliers of the final basis of the self-certifying simplex;
\item a \emph{black box}: an LP solver solves each LP on the same relaxation,
warm-started from the previous bound, and its dual solution, or its Farkas ray
if it reports infeasibility, is passed to the same interval certificate
$\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$, which is the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound written
in the homogeneous form.
\end{itemize}
The homogeneous form is therefore not what separates the engines: the
certificate, its evaluation over the box and its validity on sub-boxes are
common to all, and SoPlex itself solves ranged rows in the same form, with one
bounded logical variable per row. What differs is a simplex designed for that
certificate.

We use four black boxes. The reference is SoPlex~4.0.2 driven as Ibex drives
it, through its \texttt{SoPlex} class, in its best configuration for this use:
its own instance holding the same rows and sides with the box as column bounds,
presolving off, the nearest bound first, and the tolerance $10^{-6}$ for
systems of equations and $10^{-9}$ for optimization.\footnote{The LP interface
of Ibex also stores the $n$ variable bounds as rows, which makes each LP 16\%
more expensive on small relaxations; rows that are free for SoPlex are passed
to it empty; presolving each new relaxation costs 3\% more than it saves on LPs
of a few pivots; the farthest bound first is 3--7\% slower; $10^{-9}$ makes
SoPlex report spurious infeasibility on relaxations with nearly equal rows, and
$10^{-6}$ weakens its dual on optimization problems. Turning off scaling, or
forcing the row or column representation or the primal algorithm, did not
help, and SoPlex~7.1.6 was 1.2--1.9 times slower than 4.0.2 on a sample of
eight problems.} The second is the simplex of SoPlex driven directly through
its \texttt{SPxSolver} class, the \emph{bare simplex}: the LP stays loaded,
between bounds only the objective and the column bounds change, and nothing is
verified. It is the cheapest way to use SoPlex, and we report it alongside
SoPlex throughout. The other two are HiGHS~1.15.1 and Gurobi~12.0.3, with one
thread, the dual simplex, the basis kept between LPs and no presolve; a pass
with them costs two to five times that of SoPlex (Table~\ref{tab:bank}), so we
use them only on the bank of passes. Both forms of SoPlex have the iteration
limit $20m+200$ (Section~\ref{sec:simplex}).

\paragraph{Setting.}
Contractor and solvers are implemented in Ibex, with the two relaxations of
our previous work~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}: the affine
relaxation~\cite{ninin2015affine}, and the combined relaxation
(\texttt{compo}), which adds to the affine rows those of
X-Newton~\cite{araya2012xnewton}, one per constraint and corner. Preliminary
experiments fixed one relaxation per program: the affine relaxation for
optimization, where \texttt{compo} costs more than it saves with every engine,
and \texttt{compo} for systems of equations, where it halves the search tree.
Every engine solves the same relaxation.
Per node, the affine relaxation has a median of 5 rows (quartiles 3--9) on
optimization problems and 16 (9--30) on systems of equations after merging;
\texttt{compo} has 13 (7--20) and 56 (36--94). For systems of equations we use
the solver composition of Ibex (HC4, ACID, interval Newton when the system is
square, and the linear contractor within a fixed point with HC4) with the
LSmear bisector, on the 242 systems of the Ibex solver benchmark that some
configuration solves within 3600\,s (22 are excluded), 2 seeds, 60\,s per run.
For optimization we use IbexOpt on 240 problems, those of the Ibex optimization
benchmark that some configuration solves within 3600\,s (59 are excluded), 4
seeds, 60\,s per run. The parameters of SC were chosen on the systems and on
139 of the optimization problems, except the bound order of the optimizer,
chosen on all 240; on the other 101, which we added afterwards, SC gains as
much as on the 139. Three problems (alkyl, alkylbis, bearing) whose search
trees change by two orders of magnitude between seeds of the same
configuration are excluded from the means. We report geometric means of
ratios over the pairs of runs in which both methods finish, for runs whose
reference time exceeds 0.1, 1 and 5\,s, and the runs that finish within the
time limit (for optimization, the problems solved in all four seeds). All runs
were made on a machine with two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4 processors (2.1\,GHz, 16
cores) and 64\,GB of memory, under Linux 6.17, eight runs at a time and a
memory limit of 6\,GB per run; the code is compiled with GCC 13.3
(\texttt{-O3}) against Ibex 2.8.9 with the GAOL~4.2.0 interval library and
SoPlex~4.0.2. CPU times are process times.

\subsection{Rigor}

SC is rigorous by Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}; we also checked it against the
LP hull. On four systems (Bratu-0060, Discrete-Integralf2-13, Geneig,
Bellido), for each of the 2\,834 bounds where SC contracted more than the
polytope-hull contractor of Ibex on the same box and polytope, we solved the
bound LP over the input box with a fresh instance of SoPlex, used as an
oracle; no valid contraction can go beyond this hull. SC attained it in 98\%
of these bounds (the contractor of Ibex in 4\%) and went beyond it in 19, all
within the $10^{-9}$ feasibility tolerance of the oracle.

\subsection{Cost of a pass}\label{sec:bank}

The comparison inside the search mixes two effects: how many cells are
explored and what each pass of the contractor costs. To measure the
polytope-hull contractor alone, without the noise of the search, we built a
bank of passes: for 71 instances, one per family on systems of equations and
35 optimization problems spread over the range of running times, we recorded
the input boxes of 1\,000 calls of the contractor along the search of SC, with
the affine relaxation, and replayed each call outside the search with each
engine on the same LPs. All engines then contract every box to the same result,
and only their cost differs (Table~\ref{tab:bank}). A pass of SC costs 0.62
times that of SoPlex on systems of equations and 0.55 times on optimization
problems, and it is cheaper on all but one instance. Against the bare simplex
the ratios are 0.82 and 0.73, and SC is cheaper on 26 of the 36 systems and on
32 of the 35 optimization problems. Against HiGHS and Gurobi a pass of SC costs
0.12--0.27 times theirs. The difference lies in the cost of an LP, not in the
pivots: SC makes 28\% more pivots per LP than SoPlex on systems of equations
and 59\% more on optimization problems, where SoPlex ends many LPs at a vertex
that is already optimal for the next bound (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} To attribute the gap with SoPlex we
added to SC, at every LP and on separate vectors so that its contraction does
not change, each part of the work that SoPlex does in a call, and replayed the
bank.\footnote{Measured with an earlier version of SC, whose pass cost the
same, 0.62 and 0.55 times that of SoPlex.} In logarithmic scale the shares add
up: a refactorization at every LP accounts for 53--58\% of the gap, verifying
the solution for 21--23\%, and recomputing the vectors at the start of a solve
and copying the solution for 10--11\%. With all of them a pass of SC costs
0.91--0.96 times that of SoPlex (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}); the rest is the
machinery of a call. Driving the simplex of SoPlex directly, which keeps the
LP loaded and verifies nothing, removes about half of the same gap. About half
of the difference with SoPlex as used by Ibex is therefore its interface, and
the other half is the refactorization that its simplex makes before declaring
an optimum, which the certificate makes unnecessary.

\subsection{The whole solver}\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares SC with SoPlex and with the bare simplex inside
the search, with both relaxations. The search trees are the same within 3\% in
every comparison; what changes is the time.

With the affine relaxation, SC is 16--17\% faster than SoPlex on optimization
problems and 8--9\% faster on systems of equations, and 10\% and 4--6\% faster
than the bare simplex; it is faster than SoPlex in 93\% of the runs on systems
of equations and 92\% on optimization problems. The pass is only part of the
cost of a node, with the linearization, propagation, ACID and interval Newton,
so the 38--45\% saved per pass yields these 8--17\%. The largest gains are on
Geneig-icse ($\times0.54$ against SoPlex) and DiscreteBoundary-0200
($\times0.69$), and on hydro, hs100 and ex2\_1\_9 ($\times0.58$--$0.69$).
Against SoPlex, SC loses by more than 5\% on no system and on two optimization
problems (linear $\times1.11$, haldmads $\times1.25$).

With \texttt{compo}, which has two to four times the rows, the picture changes.
On optimization problems SC is still 5--8\% faster than SoPlex, but ties with
the bare simplex; on systems of equations it ties with SoPlex and is 7--8\%
slower than the bare simplex. The larger relaxation prunes more, for every
engine: it halves the cells on systems of equations and saves 24--35\% of them
on optimization problems, but its LPs fall where SC loses
(Section~\ref{sec:size}).

\paragraph{Where the black box loses bounds.} With the same certificate, the
engines can only differ in their multipliers. Running SC and SoPlex on the
same boxes and polytope at every node of four systems shows two mechanisms.
When SoPlex ends without an optimal basis the bound is lost, while an LP of SC
that stops early yields a weaker bound, not a lost one; with the limit that
Ibex gives SoPlex (100 iterations) this happens in 377 of 61\,815 LPs on
Geneig-icse, and with $20m+200$ in 8 of 68\,757. When both reach an optimum,
degenerate LPs have many optimal duals and each engine stops at a different
one. These differences go both ways (on Geneig-icse SoPlex contracts more in
40 of 1\,934 nodes and SC in 32; on CountercurrentReactors2-9 SC in 46 of
1\,055 and SoPlex in 22), and the mean contraction per node is the same within
$10^{-3}$ on three of the four systems.

\paragraph{Long runs.}\label{sec:long} We reran with a limit of 3600\,s and
the affine relaxation the instances on which some configuration reached the
60\,s limit (48 systems with 2 seeds, 24 optimization problems with 4 seeds);
all engines re-solve from a cold start an LP whose reported infeasibility
cannot be certified. On optimization problems the gain carries over: SC is
15\% faster than SoPlex and 5\% faster than the bare simplex, and finishes all
96 runs, against 88 for SoPlex (hs101 and hs112 unfinished in every seed) and
95 for the bare simplex. On systems of equations, where the long runs are
those of the largest relaxations, SC is 4\% faster than SoPlex and ties with
the bare simplex; it finishes 93 of 96 runs against 94. The run that SC leaves
unfinished and the others finish, of CountercurrentReactors2-26, comes from
the adaptive tuning of ACID, which in some runs settles for a long stretch on
shaving few variables, not from the linear contractor.

\subsection{The gain depends on the size of the relaxation}\label{sec:size}

relaxation, after merging. Against SoPlex, SC is faster up to 30 rows with
both relaxations and in every size with the affine one; with \texttt{compo}

On small relaxations an LP needs one to three pivots and the fixed cost of a

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why SC wins.} SC does not contract with a better polytope: all
engines solve the same relaxation and feed the same certificate. Its advantage
has two sources. The first is cost. On the same LPs a pass of SC costs
0.55--0.62 times that of SoPlex and 0.73--0.82 times that of its simplex driven
directly (Table~\ref{tab:bank}), and SC does not pivot less: it makes 28--59\%
more pivots per LP than SoPlex. What it saves is the work that the certificate
makes unnecessary, a refactorization and a verification at every LP, together
with the setup of a call and the phase~1 that a change of objective or of the
box would otherwise require (Section~\ref{sec:bank}). About half of the gap
with SoPlex as used by Ibex is its interface, and a careful integration of its
simplex recovers it; the other half is in the simplex itself. The second source
is the certificate at every basis: where the black box ends without an optimum
the bound is lost, while the multipliers of SC still certify it. With the same
iteration limit in every engine this is rare (Section~\ref{sec:main}), and
per node the engines contract alike; along the search, small differences in
either direction compound, and on Geneig-icse SC ends with 0.80 of the cells
of SoPlex. The gain of SC is therefore mostly cost: on Geneig-icse a pass of
SC costs 0.39 times that of SoPlex on the same boxes.

\paragraph{Where it loses.} Both sources are worth most when LPs are short. A
pass removes a fixed cost per call, so its weight falls as the pivots per LP
grow, and from 15--30 rows the pivots dominate (Section~\ref{sec:size}). There
SC needs more of them than SoPlex, for three reasons that multiply: the vertex
where it stops inside the optimal face of a degenerate polytope is a worse
start for the next bound, its pivoting rules need more pivots from the same
basis, and its pivots cost more, because the dual ratio test scans the whole
pivot row and $x_B$ is recomputed instead of updated. The first is the most
specific to this setting and the one we could not fix: on optimization
problems SoPlex ends 13--40\% of its LPs at a vertex that is already optimal
for the next bound, and started from those bases SC needs as few pivots, but
neither a tie-break toward the corners of the box inside the optimal face nor a
short walk inside that face (up to ten primal steps that keep the optimum) made
SC end at such vertices. The second and third are engineering, and a revised
engine with the pricing and the updates of SoPlex would remove them. Until
then, a contractor can choose the engine per node. Solving the pass with SC
when the relaxation of the previous node had at most 30 rows, and with the
bare simplex otherwise, stays within 2\% of the better engine in every
configuration of Table~\ref{tab:main}. With \texttt{compo} on systems of
equations this hybrid is 8--11\% faster than SoPlex and the best configuration
of the solver, exploring 0.47--0.56 of the cells of SC with the affine
relaxation in 0.86--0.97 of the time; on optimization problems the best remains
SC with the affine relaxation. A lower threshold fails: at 15 rows the hybrid
hands Geneig-icse to the bare simplex, which loses bounds that SC certifies,
and the run takes 2.2 times longer.

\paragraph{What did not pay.} We also measured on the whole benchmark several
ideas that use the certificate or the tableau beyond the pass, and none paid.
Reusing certificates of an ancestor box keeps them valid, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}, but not strong: tested on the slices of ACID,
the multipliers of the node itself almost never prune a slice, and those of an
ancestor prune slices that the linear contractor of the node prunes anyway.
Truncating each LP when its anytime bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}) comes
within a tolerance of what the LP can still gain saves pivots ($\times0.84$)
but weakens the contraction (cells $\times1.39$--$1.60$); only the fixed limit
of 60 pivots, which acts on a few long LPs, pays. Limiting only the first LP of
each pass, the longest, saves its pivots without moving them to the next LP,
but the first LP is too small a part of a node for this to matter.
Propagating with HC4 in the middle of a pass, when a bound has just contracted
by more than half, empties the box often, but those boxes would be emptied by
the rest of the pass anyway. Cuts at the LP optima reduce the cells
($\times0.80$--$0.88$) at a higher cost (CPU $\times1.18$--$1.31$). A Lagrangian
bound improved by monotonicity tightens the LP bound in most applications, but
costs more than it saves (CPU $\times1.08$ to $\times3.5$). Inheriting the
basis of the parent node costs only a factorization with the revised engine
and cuts the pivots of the first LP of a node by a factor of three or more,
but the first LP is a small part of the node: the time changes by less than
4\% on optimization problems and grows by 1--5\% on systems of equations.

Three components of earlier versions of SC were dropped for the same reason.
Keeping the whole tableau $T=B^{-1}\Abar$ explicitly, row by row in sparse
form, makes a pivot a Gauss--Jordan elimination with no linear system to
solve; our first versions used it on relaxations of up to 30 rows, but with the
other components in place it is 11--14\% slower than the revised engine on
systems of equations (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), and 1--2\% slower even when
limited to 30 rows. With the tableau at hand, the bounds can also be ordered by
the number of dual infeasibilities that the next LP must repair, which can be
counted without pivoting; this saved up to 2\% with the tableau, and the
nearest bound first does as well with the revised engine. Finally, the bound
problems are highly dual degenerate: with $z_k$ basic in row $r$, every column
that does not meet row $r$ has a zero reduced cost, and on some systems
(Virasoro, Katsura) 80--95\% of the dual pivots were degenerate. Perturbing the
zero reduced costs after relocation, the standard
remedy~\cite{koberstein2005dual}, saved 3--4\% on systems of equations in
earlier versions; with the other components it makes SC 1--2\% slower. The pattern is that doing more work inside a node does
not change the search tree; what changed it was what is computed: the merged
relaxation, the stability filter of the ratio test, the Farkas ray of the
infeasible row, the tolerance.

\paragraph{Limits.} SC removes the external LP solver from the contractor, not
from the optimizer: the upper-bounding of IbexOpt still solves its LPs with
SoPlex, with every engine. On problems with very narrow equalities the
upper-bounding becomes the bottleneck: on bearing, whose eight equalities are
relaxed to $|h|\le10^{-8}$, the lower bound of SC is good but feasible points
are found only in boxes of width $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$, and the search tree
changes by two orders of magnitude between seeds; we excluded it, with alkyl
and alkylbis, from the means. Relaxations of several hundred rows are still
expensive for every engine: the systems of 400 to 1\,000 variables of the
benchmark exceed the limits of 60\,s and 6\,GB with all of them. Finally, the
gain of SC is one of small relaxations: on the relaxations that make the best
solver of systems of equations, SC alone is slower than the bare simplex of
SoPlex, and the gain there comes from the hybrid. One use of the certificate
is left open: when the warm-started simplex declares an infeasibility that the
ray does not certify, SC solves the LP again from the slack basis
(Section~\ref{sec:iter}), but the multipliers of the basis where it stopped,
moved along the ray as long as the bound improves, already give a certified
bound without that second LP. Only the second LP can prove the box empty,
and these cases are rare; we have not tried it.

\section{Conclusion}

A polytope-hull contractor solves $2n$ small, closely related LPs per node and
needs a rigorous bound from each. We have shown that the certificate that makes
the bound rigorous, the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound written in the homogeneous
form of the relaxation, is also a design principle for the simplex that
produces it. Since any basis certifies, the simplex needs no verification, no
refactorization before declaring an optimum and no phase~1, can stop an LP at
any basis, and solves the $2n$ problems on one shared basis. Inside the same
contractor, with the same contraction, a pass of this simplex costs 0.55--0.62
times that of SoPlex, 0.73--0.82 times that of its simplex driven directly and
0.12--0.27 times that of HiGHS and Gurobi, and where a black box loses a bound
the simplex still certifies it. With the affine relaxation the whole
solver is 16--17\% faster than with SoPlex on optimization problems and 8--9\%
on systems of equations. The gain belongs to small relaxations, where the fixed
cost of a call dominates: beyond 15--30 rows SoPlex needs fewer pivots, mostly
because the vertices where it ends an LP are better starting points for the
next bound. A contractor that uses each engine where it is better is never
worse than either, and is the best configuration on systems of equations. How
to choose, inside the optimal face of a degenerate polytope, the vertex that
serves the next bound is the open question.
