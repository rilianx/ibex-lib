\begin{abstract}
Interval branch-and-bound solvers compute rigorous enclosures of the solutions
of nonlinear systems and of global optima. Their polytope-hull contractor
bounds each of the $n$ variables of a node over a linear relaxation by solving
$2n$ small linear programs (LPs) with a floating-point LP solver, whose dual
solutions are certified afterwards by the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound. That
bound is valid for any vector of multipliers, so it makes any LP solver safe
to use; yet a general-purpose solver still spends, inside each call, the work
of making its own answer correct. We present a self-certifying simplex (SC)
that omits that work, which the certificate makes unnecessary: it verifies
nothing, does not refactorize before declaring an optimum, needs no phase~1,
and solves the $2n$ LPs on one shared basis. A
ladder of configurations in the open-source solver Ibex, from SoPlex as Ibex
uses it, through the merging of twin rows, a tuned SoPlex and its simplex
driven directly, to SC, attributes the gain to each step. On a bank of
recorded passes, with the same LPs and the same contraction, a pass of SC on
relaxations of up to 30 rows costs 0.37--0.39 times that of SoPlex as Ibex
uses it, and 22--26\% less than the simplex of SoPlex driven directly. Inside
the search, the optimizer becomes 25\% faster than with SoPlex as Ibex uses
it. On larger relaxations SC is slower than the simplex of SoPlex, and a
hybrid that chooses the engine by the size of the relaxation keeps the best
of both.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Interval branch-and-bound solvers compute, with mathematical certainty, an
enclosure of all solutions of a system of nonlinear equations and inequalities,
or the global optimum of a constrained problem~\cite{neumaier2004complete}.
Their efficiency depends on \emph{contractors}, operators that shrink the
current box without losing solutions~\cite{chabert2009contractor}. Among them,
contractors based on linear relaxations are among the most effective: the
constraints are linearized over the box, by convex interval
Taylor forms~\cite{araya2012xnewton} or affine
arithmetic~\cite{ninin2015affine,araya2025hybridizing}, and each variable is
minimized and maximized over the resulting polytope. In Ibex this is the
polytope-hull contractor, the linear part of X-Newton, which we introduced
in~\cite{araya2012xnewton}; we later combined the affine and Taylor-based
relaxations~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}.

Each call solves up to $2n$ linear programs, and the rigor of the result cannot
come from the LP solver, which works in floating point. Current implementations
therefore treat the LP solver as a black box and certify its output a
posteriori: the dual solution is turned into a safe bound by the
Neumaier--Shcherbina procedure~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, and an infeasibility
claim is accepted only if the returned Farkas ray passes the same verification.
This design has a cost that is easy to miss: the $2n$ LPs of a node are small
and closely related, typically two or three pivots apart, and each is a
separate call to a general-purpose solver, which sets up its vectors,
refactorizes the basis before declaring an optimum and verifies its solution;
on these LPs that work exceeds the pivots themselves
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

The certificate already makes any LP solver safe to use: with the relaxation
written in a homogeneous form, with one interval-bounded slack per row,
\emph{any} multiplier vector yields a valid bound on the box and on every
sub-box, whatever solver produced it. But a black box cannot take advantage of
this, because the work it does to make its answer correct is internal to each
call. We design the simplex around the certificate instead: since its
multipliers never need to be correct, it verifies nothing and does not
refactorize before declaring an optimum. Since every basis can also be made dual feasible by relocating its
nonbasic variables, each of the $2n$ bound problems, and each problem after
the box contracts, starts without a phase~1 (the search for a feasible
starting basis), and the $2n$ problems share one basis. We call this simplex
\emph{self-certifying} (SC). How much it is worth depends on the size of the
relaxation and on what else the contractor does, and measuring that is part of
the contribution.

\paragraph{Positioning.} The certificate itself is not new. That any
multiplier vector yields a valid bound once the residual is evaluated in
interval arithmetic is the principle of Neumaier and
Shcherbina~\cite{neumaier2004safe}. Domes and Neumaier~\cite{domes2012rigorous}
applied it to filtering with linear relaxations: they solve the bound LPs with
an external solver, collect the multipliers, and solve the preconditioned
interval system row by row. In previous work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning} we
wrote the relaxation in the homogeneous form used here and showed that the
optimal preconditioner for an interval Gauss--Seidel projection is given by the
dual solutions of the bound LPs; the equivalent valid inequalities appear in
the optimization-based bound tightening of Gleixner et
al.~\cite{gleixner2017three}, and both works reuse them in sub-boxes. In all
these approaches the multipliers are a by-product of an external solver and
certification is a post-processing step, whose cost leads to applying it to a
few promising variables~\cite{domes2012rigorous}, or to recomputing it only
periodically and reusing it in between~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}. What
changes with SC is \emph{where the multipliers come from and how often a usable
certificate is available}: the simplex itself produces them, at every basis.
On the solver side, Yamamura and
coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} observed that, after a
bisection, the optimal tableau of the parent remains dual feasible for the
child, so that an LP emptiness test can be resumed by the dual simplex method
with few or no pivots per box; their warm start concerns a single feasibility
LP on a fixed relaxation, without rigorous certification, and SC extends it to
the $2n$ objectives of the bound problems. Bound tightening by optimization is
also central in MINLP solvers~\cite{belotti2009branching,gleixner2017three},
where rigor is not required; the heuristic of skipping bounds already attained
by a primal point, used by Ibex, comes from Baharev, Achterberg and
R\'ev~\cite{baharev2009distillation}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A simplex designed for the certificate.} SC omits the
verification, the refactorization before an optimum and the phase~1 of a
general-purpose solver, and solves the $2n$ problems of a node on one shared
basis (Section~\ref{sec:simplex}). The properties it relies on, validity of
the certificate for any multipliers, exactness at an optimum and dual
feasibility for free, follow from the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound and LP
duality; we state them in Section~\ref{sec:dfb} because the design rests on
them, not as new results.
\item \emph{An attribution of the gain.} A ladder of configurations inside the
same contractor of Ibex, from SoPlex as Ibex uses it, through the merging of
twin rows (a simple preprocessing that halves the relaxation of square systems
for every engine), a tuned SoPlex and its simplex driven directly, to SC and a
hybrid, measures what each step contributes, separately on optimization problems and systems of equations and
on small and large relaxations: on a bank of recorded passes, where every
rung solves the same LPs, and inside the search, with one instance per family
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \emph{The boundary of the approach.} SC pays on relaxations of up to
about 30 rows, where the fixed cost of a call dominates; beyond, its pivots
cost more than those of SoPlex. We explain why, and give a hybrid contractor
that chooses the engine per node by the size of the relaxation
(Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Interval solvers and linear relaxations.}
Interval branch and bound alternates bisection with contraction. Ibex, on
which we build, composes constraint propagation (HC4~\cite{benhamou1999revising}),
shaving-based consistencies such as ACID~\cite{neveu2015adaptive}, the interval
Newton operator for square systems~\cite{neumaier1990interval}, and contractors
based on linear relaxations~\cite{chabert2009contractor}. The polytope-hull
contractor computes a linear relaxation of the constraints over the box and
bounds each variable over it; in X-Newton~\cite{araya2012xnewton} the
relaxation comes from convex interval Taylor forms, and the affine arithmetic
relaxation of Ninin et al.~\cite{ninin2015affine} is an alternative which we
later combined with it~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}. QUAD~\cite{lebbah2005quad}
and GloptLab~\cite{domes2012rigorous} use linear relaxations in the same way.
In previous work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning} we wrote the relaxation in the
homogeneous form used here and showed that the optimal preconditioner for a
Gauss--Seidel projection over it is given by the dual solutions of the bound
LPs. Yamamura and coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} solve an LP
emptiness test at each box of a bisection search and resume it by the dual
simplex method from the parent's tableau.

\paragraph{Rigorous bounds for linear programs.}
A floating-point LP solver returns approximate solutions, and two lines of
work make its results rigorous. The first certifies them a posteriori.
Neumaier and Shcherbina~\cite{neumaier2004safe} showed that any approximate
dual vector yields a safe bound once the residual is evaluated in interval
arithmetic, and that an approximate Farkas ray proves infeasibility under the
same verification; Jansson~\cite{jansson2004rigorous} gave rigorous lower and
upper bounds based on the same principle, implemented in
Lurupa~\cite{keil2006computational}. In filtering, Domes and
Neumaier~\cite{domes2012rigorous} apply this certificate to the bound LPs of a
linear relaxation, collecting the multipliers from an external solver for a
few promising variables. The second line solves the LP exactly, in rational
arithmetic~\cite{applegate2007exact}, or to arbitrary precision by iterative
refinement~\cite{gleixner2016iterative}, as needed in exact mixed-integer
programming~\cite{cook2013hybrid}. Both treat the LP solver as given. The
present work keeps the a posteriori certificate, which costs no more than one
matrix--vector product, and changes the solver instead.

\paragraph{Optimization-based bound tightening.}
Minimizing and maximizing each variable over a relaxation is also a standard
bound tightening technique in global optimization, where rigor is not
required: range reduction in BARON~\cite{ryoo1996branch}, and
optimization-based bound tightening in Couenne~\cite{belotti2009branching} and
SCIP. Gleixner et al.~\cite{gleixner2017three} make it cheaper by filtering
bounds already attained by feasible points, by ordering the LPs greedily, and
by turning the dual solutions into valid inequalities that are propagated in
the subtree. The skipping rule used by Ibex comes from Baharev, Achterberg and
R\'ev~\cite{baharev2009distillation}: a bound attained by a primal point found
earlier needs no LP, and the next LP is warm-started from the previous one.

\paragraph{Simplex techniques.}
Our simplex uses standard ingredients of modern implementations, described in
detail by Koberstein~\cite{koberstein2005dual} for the dual method: the
bounded-variable dual simplex with Harris' ratio test~\cite{harris1973devex},
and the revised method with a sparse LU factorization of the basis updated by
Forrest and Tomlin's method~\cite{forrest1972updated}, as in
SoPlex~\cite{wunderling1996paralleler}. We also tried the long-step (bound
flipping) ratio test~\cite{maros2003generalized} and devex
pricing~\cite{harris1973devex}, an approximation of steepest
edge~\cite{forrest1992steepest}; on the LPs of a pass they did not pay.
What is specific to our setting is not any of these techniques but their use
for $2n$ closely related LPs over the same constraints, with a certificate
attached to every basis.

\section{The contractor}\label{sec:contractor}

This section describes the contractor independently of the engine that
solves its LPs: the relaxation and its certificate
(Section~\ref{sec:dfb}), the pass over the bounds
(Section~\ref{sec:pass}) and the merging of twin rows
(Section~\ref{sec:merge}). Section~\ref{sec:simplex} then describes SC, and
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} compares it with external LP solvers inside this
same contractor. Only SC is new; the rest is the common ground on which the
engines are compared.

\subsection{The homogeneous relaxation and its certificate}\label{sec:dfb}

Let $\iv{x}\in\IR^n$ be the current box. A linearizer returns a matrix
$A\in\R^{m\times n}$ of floating-point numbers and an interval vector
$\iv{b}\in\IR^m$, possibly with infinite bounds, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relax}
x \in \iv{x} \text{ is a solution} \;\Longrightarrow\; A x \in \iv{b}.
\end{equation}
We introduce one slack per row, $s = Ax$, and write the relaxation in the
homogeneous form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:homog}
\Abar z = 0,\qquad z = (x,s)\in \iv{z} := \iv{x}\times\iv{s},\qquad
\Abar := [\,A \;\; -I_m\,],
\end{equation}
where $\iv{s}_i := \iv{b}_i \cap \square(A_{i:}\,\iv{x})$ and
$\square(\cdot)$ denotes evaluation in interval arithmetic. We call
$P(\iv{x}) := \{ z\in\iv{z} : \Abar z = 0\}$ the \emph{relaxation polytope}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:relax}
If $x^*\in\iv{x}$ is a solution, then $(x^*, Ax^*)\in P(\iv{x})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$A_{i:}x^*\in\iv{b}_i$ by~\eqref{eq:relax} and $A_{i:}x^*\in\square(A_{i:}\iv{x})$
by the inclusion property of interval arithmetic; the equation holds by
construction of $s$.
\end{proof}

The certificate combines the $m$ rows of $\Abar z=0$ linearly, with one
\emph{multiplier} per row, into a single equation $\gamma^Tz=0$ that every point
of the polytope satisfies. Any vector of multipliers $\lambda\in\R^m$ gives a
valid equation; the best ones for bounding $z_k$ are the dual solutions of the
LP that minimizes or maximizes $z_k$ over the polytope, and the simplex of Section~\ref{sec:simplex}
produces them.

\begin{definition}[Certificate]\label{def:cert}
For a vector of multipliers $\lambda\in\R^m$, let $\iv{\gamma}\in\IR^{n+m}$ be an interval vector that
contains $\gamma := \Abar^T\lambda$, computed in outward-rounded interval
arithmetic. For $k\in\{1,\dots,n+m\}$ with $0\notin\iv{\gamma}_k$, the
\emph{certified enclosure} of $z_k$ is
\[
\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z}) := -\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j\neq k}\iv{\gamma}_j\,\iv{z}_j\Big) \Big/ \iv{\gamma}_k ,
\]
and the \emph{residual} is $\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z}) := \sum_{j}\iv{\gamma}_j\,\iv{z}_j$.
\end{definition}

The idea is a single line. Every point $z$ of the polytope satisfies
$\Abar z=0$, hence $\gamma^T z=\lambda^T\Abar z=0$ for any $\lambda$, that is,
$\gamma_k z_k=-\sum_{j\neq k}\gamma_j z_j$. Bounding the right-hand side over
the box in interval arithmetic, and dividing by $\gamma_k$, encloses $z_k$; and
if the residual $\iv{R}$ excludes zero, no point of the box can satisfy
$\gamma^Tz=0$, so the polytope is empty. A contraction step therefore replaces
$\iv{z}_k$ by $\iv{z}_k\cap\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$, and an emptiness test
checks $0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$. All statements below hold for the
floating-point $A$ returned by the linearizer; rounding in $\iv{\gamma}$ and
$\iv{C}_k$ is covered by outward rounding.

\begin{proposition}[Validity]\label{prop:valid}
Let $\lambda\in\R^m$ be arbitrary. For every $z\in P(\iv{x})$ and every $k$
with $0\notin\iv{\gamma}_k$, $z_k\in\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$; if
$0\notin\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$, then $P(\iv{x})=\emptyset$ and $\iv{x}$
contains no solution. Both claims hold on every sub-box $\iv{x}'\subseteq\iv{x}$
with the matrix $\Abar$ computed on $\iv{x}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $z\in P(\iv{x})$ and the exact $\gamma=\Abar^T\lambda\in\iv{\gamma}$,
$\gamma^Tz=\lambda^T\Abar z=0$; both claims follow from the inclusion property
of interval arithmetic and Lemma~\ref{lem:relax}. The linearization on
$\iv{x}$ remains valid on $\iv{x}'$, so the polytope over $\iv{x}'$ lies in
$P(\iv{x})$.
\end{proof}

No property of $\lambda$ is used: it need not be optimal, feasible or
accurate, and errors in it can only weaken the enclosure. Written back in the
variables $x$, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda,\iv{z})$ is the Neumaier--Shcherbina safe
lower bound of $\min x_k$ subject to $Ax\in\iv{b}$, $x\in\iv{x}$, with
approximate dual $\lambda$~\cite{neumaier2004safe}, and the row-wise solution
of the preconditioned system of Domes and Neumaier~\cite[\S5]{domes2012rigorous};
validity on sub-boxes is used by Gleixner et al.~\cite{gleixner2017three} and
in our earlier work~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}. The homogeneous form,
introduced in~\cite{reyes2023preconditioning}, adds one detail: each slack is
bounded by $\square(A_{i:}\iv{x})$ even when a side of $\iv{b}_i$ is infinite,
so a multiplier of the ``wrong'' sign on a one-sided row gives a weaker but
finite bound, and literally any $\lambda$ certifies. Two standard facts
complete what the design uses. At an optimal basis of $\mathrm{LP}_k$ with
$z_k$ basic, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda^*,\iv{z})$ equals the optimal value in
exact arithmetic, by LP duality~\cite[Prop.~1]{reyes2023preconditioning}; in
floating point the excess is at most $\sum_j\wid(\iv{\gamma}_j)\magn(\iv{z}_j)$
plus the effect of the inaccuracy of $\lambda$, as for the
Neumaier--Shcherbina bound. And every basis can be made dual feasible for
free~\cite{koberstein2005dual}:

\begin{proposition}[Dual-feasible start]\label{prop:start}
The slack basis $B=-I_m$ is dual feasible for the bound problem of every
structural variable $z_k$, $k\le n$, with $\inff\iv{z}_k$ finite. If every
$\iv{z}_j$ is bounded, any basis becomes dual feasible after placing each
nonbasic $z_j$ at its lower bound if its reduced cost $d_j>0$ and at its upper
bound if $d_j<0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With the slack basis the cost of every basic variable is zero, so $\lambda=0$
and $d=e_k$, and $z_k$ at its lower bound has the right sign. In general $d$
does not depend on where the nonbasic variables sit, and the relocation
enforces the sign conditions whenever the required bound is finite.
\end{proof}

When the objective changes from one bound to the next, or the box contracts,
relocation thus restores dual feasibility without a phase~1, which is what
lets one basis serve the $2n$ problems. Yamamura and
coworkers~\cite{yamamura2001dual,yamamura2006dual} used the case of a box
change to resume an LP emptiness test after a bisection. Section~\ref{sec:simplex}
explains how the simplex produces $\lambda$.

\paragraph{Why not a rigorous simplex.}
Validity does not depend on how $\lambda$ was computed: any vector of
multipliers gives a valid bound, and a poor one only gives a weaker bound. Rigor is
therefore needed only in the final evaluation of $\iv{\gamma}$ and
$\iv{C}_k$, not in the pivoting. Carrying intervals through the pivots
instead pays the overestimation of interval Gaussian
elimination~\cite{neumaier1990interval}, whose widths grow with the number of
pivots, and solving each LP exactly in rational arithmetic, or to high
precision by iterative refinement~\cite{applegate2007exact,gleixner2016iterative},
buys an exactness that the bound does not need, at a cost that $2n$ LPs per
node cannot afford. An early version of our contractor pivoted in interval
arithmetic: on a one-box benchmark it had to rebuild the tableau from the
original relaxation 731 times to control the width growth, and pivoting in
floating point with certification of the final multipliers only was 2.8 times
faster with essentially the same contraction (mean 24.0\% against 24.6\%).

\subsection{One pass over the bounds}\label{sec:pass}

At each node the relaxation is computed, written in the
form~\eqref{eq:homog}, and loaded once. The contractor then makes one pass over
the up to $2n$ bound problems
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpk}
\mathrm{LP}_k:\quad \min\; z_k \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Abar z = 0,\; z\in\iv{z},
\end{equation}
and their maximizations, obtained with $-z_k$, each LP warm-started from the
final basis of the previous one. When an LP ends, its bound is certified once,
from the multipliers of the final basis, and the box is contracted before the
next LP starts. Two rules decide which LPs are solved and in which order. A
bound is \emph{skipped} when a primal point obtained earlier in the pass
already attains it, since its LP would return that value; this rule comes from
the polytope-hull contractor of Ibex~\cite{baharev2009distillation}. Among the
remaining bounds, the contractor of Ibex solves first the one farthest from
the last primal point; we solve first the nearest one, whose optimum lies
closest to the vertex where the previous LP ended, so that the next LP needs
fewer pivots. This order is the best one for every engine we compare.

If the residual $\iv{R}(\lambda,\iv{z})$ of an LP excludes zero, the node is
empty and the pass stops. No other outcome stops it. An LP that ends without
an optimum, at its iteration limit, still has the multipliers of the basis
where it stopped, which certify a weaker but valid bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}); an LP that reports an infeasibility whose ray
fails the residual test leaves its bound uncontracted, and is solved once more
from a cold start. The polytope-hull contractor of Ibex~2.8.9 instead
abandons the pass in both cases; we corrected it, the correction has been
submitted to the Ibex distribution, and every engine of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} uses it, including the reference. Ibex limits
SoPlex to 100 iterations per LP; the other configurations, SC included, use
$20m+200$, where $m$ is the number of rows.

\subsection{Merging twin rows}\label{sec:merge}

Rows of the relaxation that are proportional, within a relative tolerance of
$10^{-9}$, are merged into one row whose slack interval is the intersection of
their scaled intervals; the polytope does not change. The affine linearizer
writes each equation as two one-sided rows with the same normal, so merging
halves $m$ on square systems, and the combined relaxation of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} writes some rows twice, with opposite signs, far
apart in the matrix. Merging is done once per node, before any LP, and gives a
smaller LP to SC and to an external solver alike.

\section{A simplex for the \texorpdfstring{$2n$}{2n} bound problems}\label{sec:simplex}

For each structural variable $x_k$ the pass of Section~\ref{sec:pass} needs
the two bounds of $\mathrm{LP}_k$~\eqref{eq:lpk}. A basis is a set of $m$ columns
of $\Abar$ forming a nonsingular matrix $B$; with cost vector $c=\pm e_k$, the
multipliers are $\lambda^T = c_B^T B^{-1}$ and the reduced costs
$d = c - \Abar^T\lambda$. For the bounded-variable simplex, a basis with each
nonbasic variable at one of its bounds is \emph{dual feasible} if $d_j\ge 0$ for
nonbasic variables at their lower bound and $d_j\le 0$ for those at their upper
bound, and \emph{primal feasible} if the basic variables lie within their
bounds. This section describes how the simplex solves the $2n$ problems of a
node; the design choices are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{What the certificate makes unnecessary.} Since the certificate of
Section~\ref{sec:dfb} is valid for any $\lambda$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}),
the simplex never has to return a correct solution, only a good basis. A
general-purpose LP solver, before declaring an optimum, refactorizes the basis
to remove the error accumulated by the updates of its factorization,
recomputes its vectors and verifies the solution, and the contractor then
verifies it once more through the certificate. SC uses the final basis as it
is: an inaccurate $\lambda$ yields a weaker bound, never an invalid one, so the
error of the factorization is controlled only as far as pivoting requires.
Likewise, since every basis can be made dual feasible
(Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), no phase~1 is needed when the objective changes
or the box contracts, and the $2n$ problems can share one basis.
Section~\ref{sec:bank} measures what these savings are worth against SoPlex.

The rest of this section describes the simplex that results: how each LP
starts from the previous one (Section~\ref{sec:warm}), the iterations
(Section~\ref{sec:iter}), the factorization of the basis
(Section~\ref{sec:engines}), and how the certificate is computed when an LP
ends (Section~\ref{sec:certif}).

\subsection{Starting and warm-starting}\label{sec:warm}

The first LP of a pass has no previous basis. It starts from the slack basis
$B=-I_m$, which is dual feasible for every $\mathrm{LP}_k$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), and is solved by the dual method, with no
phase~1.

Between two consecutive LPs of the pass the objective changes, and the
contracted bound changes the box. Two warm starts are possible from the
previous optimal basis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{dual}: relocate every nonbasic variable to the bound indicated by
the sign of its new reduced cost, $z_k$ included, which restores dual
feasibility (Proposition~\ref{prop:start}), and continue with the dual method;
the certified bound is then available, and nondecreasing, at every iteration
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mono});
\item \emph{primal}: keep the nonbasic variables where they are; the basis
remains primal feasible, up to the contracted bound, and is reoptimized with
primal steps, which is the usual warm start of an LP solver.
\end{itemize}
Under the dual method the certificate is available at every iteration, and it
never gets worse:

\begin{proposition}[Monotone certified bound]\label{prop:mono}
Run the dual simplex method on $\mathrm{LP}_k$ from a dual-feasible basis. At
every iteration $t$ in which $z_k$ is basic, $\inff\iv{C}_k(\lambda_t,\iv{z})$
is a valid lower bound on $z_k$ over $P(\iv{x})$ and, in exact arithmetic, it
is nondecreasing in $t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Validity is Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}. With $z_k$ basic, $d=e_k-\Abar^T\lambda_t$
has $d_k=0$, so $\gamma_k=1$, $\gamma_j=-d_j$ for $j\neq k$, and
$\inff\iv{C}_k=\sum_{j\neq k}\min_{z_j\in\iv{z}_j}d_jz_j=\min_{z\in\iv{z}}d^Tz$,
the Lagrangian dual function of $\mathrm{LP}_k$ at $\lambda_t$, which the
iterates of the dual simplex method do not decrease.
\end{proof}

An LP stopped at its iteration limit under the dual method therefore keeps a
bound no weaker than at its start (Section~\ref{sec:pass}).
Both reach an optimal basis, so the certificate at termination is exact
(Section~\ref{sec:dfb}) and the pruning is the same; whether the node is
empty is decided in the first LP, common to both. They differ only in cost.

The cost depends on how far apart the optima of consecutive bounds are. An
optimal vertex is given by a basis and by the bound on which each nonbasic
variable rests, and a nonbasic variable that must move to its opposite bound
costs the two warm starts differently: the primal one pays an iteration for it,
the dual one moves it for free, since relocation is not a pivot. Relocation has
its own cost, however, paid once per LP: it changes the values of the nonbasic
variables, and the basic values must be recomputed from the factorization.
So the primal warm start is cheaper when few variables change side, and the
dual one when many do.

How many change side depends on the rows. On systems of equations every row
comes from an equation and is a narrow slab: the interval of its slack is, on
average, 13\% of the range of the row over the box, so the optima of
consecutive bounds often rest on opposite faces of a slab. On optimization
problems the slack intervals cover 48\% of the range, and few rows change side.
Table~\ref{tab:sides} measures it over the benchmarks of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. The basis changes by 10--17\% in both programs,
but a median of 1.9 nonbasic variables per LP change side on systems of
equations, against 0.3 on optimization problems. The iterations follow: on
systems the primal warm start makes 5.1 per LP against 3.1 for the dual one,
about one more per side change; on optimization problems it makes 2.1
against 2.5.

In time, the two effects balance differently in each program. On
optimization problems the primal warm start pivots as little as the dual one
and saves the relocation: in the whole solver, the dual warm start is 4\%
slower. On systems of equations the two extra
primal pivots per LP cost about as much as the relocation they avoid, and both
warm starts take the same time. SC therefore uses the primal warm start in the
optimizer, where the first LP of each pass is still solved by the dual method,
and the dual warm start in the solver of systems of equations. There the two
cost the same and the choice matters little; we keep the dual one so that the
whole pass runs the dual method of its first LP, with primal steps only as a
fallback. With the larger relaxation of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} neither warm start wins on every system, and SC
chooses per run: it alternates the two on the first nodes, keeps the one with
the lower cost per LP, measured as a moving average, and tries the other again
every 25 nodes. With the primal warm start, primal steps are also the fallback
when relocation is impossible because the bound indicated by a reduced cost is
infinite; with the dual one, SC takes no primal steps, and an LP that ends
with such a variable gives a valid, possibly weaker bound.

\subsection{Simplex iterations}\label{sec:iter}

We recall the bounded-variable simplex in the notation of
$\mathrm{LP}_k$~\eqref{eq:lpk}, following~\cite{koberstein2005dual}. Let $B$ be
the basis matrix, $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ the basic and nonbasic
index sets, and $x_N$ the nonbasic variables, each at one of its bounds; the
basic values are $x_B = -B^{-1}\Abar_{\mathcal{N}}x_N$, since the right-hand
side of~\eqref{eq:homog} is zero. Every iteration exchanges one basic and one
nonbasic variable and needs three vectors:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{pivot column} $\alpha_q = B^{-1}\Abar_{:q}$ of the entering
variable $z_q$, obtained by solving $B\alpha_q = \Abar_{:q}$ (forward
transformation, FTRAN);
\item the \emph{pivot row} $\alpha_r^T = e_r^T B^{-1}\Abar$ of the variable
leaving at position $r$, obtained by solving $B^T\rho_r = e_r$ (backward
transformation, BTRAN) and then computing $\alpha_r^T = \rho_r^T\Abar$;
\item the step lengths: a primal step $\theta_p$ for $x_B$ and a dual step
$\theta_d = d_q/\alpha_{rq}$ for $(\lambda,d)$.
\end{itemize}
The updates are then
\[
x_B \leftarrow x_B - \theta_p\,\alpha_q,\qquad
\lambda \leftarrow \lambda + \theta_d\,\rho_r,\qquad
d \leftarrow d - \theta_d\,\alpha_r ,
\]
with $z_q$ taking position $r$ of the basis. The certificate needs $\lambda$,
which is therefore always at hand.

\paragraph{Dual steps.} In a dual iteration the basis is dual feasible and
some basic variable violates its bounds. The leaving variable is the basic
variable with the largest violation. The entering variable comes from the
ratio test over the pivot row: among the nonbasic $z_j$ whose reduced cost
would change sign in the wrong direction, the one with the smallest
$|d_j/\alpha_{rj}|$ keeps the basis dual feasible; among the candidates within
a relative band of that ratio we take the largest $|\alpha_{rj}|$ (Harris'
rule), which avoids tiny pivots.

\paragraph{Rules that did not pay.} We also implemented the standard
refinements of the dual method~\cite{koberstein2005dual}: devex
pricing~\cite{harris1973devex} for the leaving variable, the long-step (bound
flipping) ratio test~\cite{maros2003generalized}, and a limit of 60 pivots per
LP, which the certificate makes safe (Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}). Each helps
on some families and hurts on others: together they make the Bratu systems
1.3--1.5 times faster and the BroydenBanded ones up to 2.6 times slower, and
on average they leave the time unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). SC
therefore uses the textbook rules above. On the small LPs of a pass, the
choice of pivots matters less than the work that surrounds them.

\paragraph{Primal steps.} In a primal iteration the basis is primal feasible
and some nonbasic variable has a reduced cost of the wrong sign; it enters
(Dantzig's rule: largest $|d_j|$), and the ratio test over the pivot column
$\alpha_q$ finds the first basic variable to reach a bound, or lets $z_q$ move
to its own opposite bound. Pivots below a stability threshold are filtered
out before choosing the leaving variable; without this filter a rejected
pivot was indistinguishable from optimality, and 40\% of the reported optima
were not dual feasible. SC takes primal steps only with the primal warm start:
to reoptimize after each bound, and to repair the nonbasic variables that
could not be relocated.

\paragraph{Tolerances and infinite bounds.} The primal and dual feasibility
tolerances are $10^{-9}$, relative to the magnitude of the value tested; with
the combined relaxation of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, whose nearly parallel
rows make the LPs degenerate, we use $10^{-6}$ in the solver of systems of
equations. A tolerance is a matter of efficiency, not of rigor. A tolerance too
wide accepts an empty polytope as feasible, which only delays the pruning, and
weakens the contraction: with $10^{-6}$ and the affine relaxation, SC explores
twice the cells on Geneig-icse. A tolerance too tight makes the warm-started
simplex report infeasibility on boxes that are not empty; the ray then fails
the residual test, and SC solves that LP again from the slack basis instead of
discarding it. Slacks may have an
infinite side; a nonbasic variable always sits at a finite bound, the other
one if the bound indicated by its reduced cost is infinite, and at zero if it
is free.

\subsection{The engine}\label{sec:engines}

SC keeps a sparse LU factorization $B = LU$, computed by the factorization
code of SoPlex, and obtains each iteration's vectors by triangular solves:
FTRAN solves $Ly=\Abar_{:q}$ and then $U\alpha_q = y$, BTRAN solves $U^Tw=e_r$
and then $L^T\rho_r=w$. Since the basis changes by one column per iteration,
the factors are not recomputed but updated: in the Forrest--Tomlin
update~\cite{forrest1972updated} the new column replaces column $r$ of $U$,
which breaks its triangular shape; a permutation moves it to the end, and one
row elimination, stored as a sparse row transformation appended to $L$,
restores the triangle. The update reuses the vector $y$ of the last FTRAN, so
it costs about one more solve. Updates make the factors grow, and SC
refactorizes $B$ from scratch every 50 pivots. The FTRAN of the entering
column is computed once per iteration and serves both the Forrest--Tomlin
update and the update of $x_B$, which is
recomputed from scratch every 32 pivots to limit drift. The reduced costs and
$\lambda$ are updated with the pivot row as above. An iteration costs one
FTRAN, one BTRAN, one product $\rho_r^T\Abar$ and the update, all
proportional to the number of nonzeros involved.

\subsection{Certification}\label{sec:certif}

When an LP terminates, $\lambda$ is read from the final basis, where it is
kept updated with the pivot row. The certificate $\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$ is computed
once per bound in outward-rounded interval arithmetic, over the rows with
$\lambda_i\neq 0$, and $\iv{C}_k$ and $\iv{R}$ follow in $O(n+m)$
(Definition~\ref{def:cert}). When a dual iteration finds a basic variable out
of its bounds and no entering variable in its row $r$, the polytope is empty,
and the row $r$ of $B^{-1}$ is a Farkas ray: SC takes $\lambda=\rho_r$, and the
residual test turns it into a proof of emptiness. The simplex itself never needs to be correct:
a wrong pivot or an inaccurate $\lambda$ yields a weaker bound, never an
invalid one (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}). Certifying a bound costs the same
as the Neumaier--Shcherbina post-processing of an external solver's dual,
since both compute $\Abar^T\lambda$ over the rows with $\lambda_i\neq 0$ plus
$O(n+m)$ operations: 1.3--3.1\,$\mu$s per LP on three optimization problems,
against 1.9--3.0\,$\mu$s plus 1.2--1.5\,$\mu$s to extract the solution from
the external solver. The difference between the approaches is not the cost of
a certificate but where $\lambda$ comes from.

\section{Experimental evaluation}\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{A ladder of configurations.}
All configurations run inside the \emph{same} contractor: the same pass over
the $2n$ bounds, with the same skipping rule, the same certificate
$\iv{\gamma}=\Abar^T\lambda$ applied to whatever multipliers the engine returns
(its dual solution, or its Farkas ray if it reports infeasibility), and the
same policy when an LP ends without an optimum: the multipliers where it
stopped certify a weaker bound, and the pass continues
(Section~\ref{sec:pass}). The configurations differ in one step at a time:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[R0] \emph{SoPlex as used by Ibex}: SoPlex~4.0.2 through the LP interface
of Ibex, which stores the $n$ variable bounds as rows, with presolving on, the
tolerance $10^{-9}$, the bounds in their natural order and at most 100
iterations per LP;
\item[R1] \emph{+ merging twin rows} (Section~\ref{sec:merge});
\item[R2] \emph{+ a tuned SoPlex}: its own instance holding the box as column
bounds, presolving off, the tolerance $10^{-6}$ for systems of equations and
$10^{-9}$ for optimization, the nearest bound first and the limit $20m+200$;%
\footnote{Each of these choices is the best one for SoPlex on these LPs:
presolving each new relaxation costs more than it saves on LPs of a few
pivots, $10^{-9}$ makes SoPlex report spurious infeasibility on relaxations
with nearly equal rows, and $10^{-6}$ weakens its dual on optimization
problems. Turning off scaling, or forcing the row or column representation or
the primal algorithm, did not help, and SoPlex~7.1.6 was 1.2--1.9 times slower
than 4.0.2 on a sample of eight problems.}
\item[R3] \emph{the bare simplex}: the simplex of SoPlex driven directly through
its \texttt{SPxSolver} class, the LP kept loaded, only the objective and the
column bounds changed between LPs, nothing verified; it is the cheapest way
to use SoPlex;
\item[R4] \emph{SC} (Section~\ref{sec:simplex});
\item[R5] \emph{the hybrid}: SC on relaxations of at most 30 rows, the bare
simplex beyond.
\end{enumerate}
Each rung is compared with R0. HiGHS~1.15.1 and Gurobi~12.0.3, with one
thread, the dual simplex, the basis kept between LPs and no presolve, cost
2.3--4.6 times as much per pass as the tuned SoPlex, so we leave them out of
the ladder.

\paragraph{Setting.}
Contractor and solvers are implemented in Ibex 2.8.9, with the two relaxations
of our previous work~\cite{araya2025hybridizing}: the affine
relaxation~\cite{ninin2015affine}, and the combined relaxation
(\texttt{compo}), which adds to the affine rows those of
X-Newton~\cite{araya2012xnewton}, one per constraint and corner. Preliminary
experiments fixed the relaxation of each program: the affine one for
optimization, where \texttt{compo} costs more than it saves with every engine,
and \texttt{compo} for systems of equations, where it halves the search tree.
Per node, after merging, the affine relaxation has a median of 5 rows
(quartiles 3--9) on optimization problems and 16 (9--30) on systems of
equations; \texttt{compo} has 56 (36--94) on systems. For systems of
equations we use the solver composition of Ibex (HC4, ACID, interval Newton
when the system is square, and the linear contractor within a fixed point with
HC4) with the LSmear bisector, on the 242 systems of the Ibex solver benchmark
that some configuration solves within 3600\,s, 2 seeds, 60\,s per run; for
optimization, IbexOpt on the 240 problems of the Ibex optimization benchmark
that some configuration solves within 3600\,s, 4 seeds, 60\,s per run. All
runs were made on two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4 processors (2.1\,GHz, 16 cores)
with 64\,GB of memory, under Linux 6.17, eight runs at a time with at most
6\,GB each; the code is compiled with GCC 13.3 (\texttt{-O3}) with the
GAOL~4.2.0 interval library. CPU times are process times.

these relaxations to the bare simplex. Devex pricing, the long-step ratio test

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why SC wins.} SC does not contract with a better polytope: every
rung solves the same relaxation and feeds the same certificate, and from R1 on
they contract alike. Its advantage is the cost of an LP. The ladder separates
two parts of what a call of SoPlex as Ibex uses it costs: the interface and
its options (R0 to R3), which a careful integration of SoPlex removes, and
what the simplex of SoPlex itself does at every LP, which only a simplex
designed for the certificate can omit. SC pivots more than SoPlex, but it
neither refactorizes before declaring an optimum nor verifies its answer,
needs no phase~1 when the objective or the box changes, and keeps one basis
for the whole pass. On small relaxations, where an LP needs two or three
pivots, that is a quarter of the cost of a pass of the best use of SoPlex.

\paragraph{Where it loses.} The saving is a fixed cost per LP, so its weight
falls as the pivots per LP grow, and beyond 30 rows they dominate. There SC
needs more pivots than SoPlex, for three reasons that multiply. On
Katsurabis-14 with \texttt{compo}, the clearest case, a pass of SC cost 2.7
times that of the tuned SoPlex on the same LPs. The starting point,
$\times1.5$: started from the final basis of SoPlex instead of its own, SC
drops from 33 to 22 pivots per LP, because on a large degenerate polytope the
optimum of a bound is a face, and SoPlex stops inside it at vertices that serve
the next bound better. The pivoting rules, $\times1.25$: 22 against 17.7
pivots from the same basis. The cost per pivot, $\times1.4$ (19.4 against
14.0\,$\mu$s), mostly the dual ratio test over the whole pivot row and
recomputing $x_B$, which SoPlex updates. The first reason is the most specific
to this setting and the one we could not fix: on optimization problems SoPlex
ends 13--40\% of its LPs at a vertex that is already optimal for the next
bound, and started from those bases SC needs as few pivots, but neither a
tie-break toward the corners of the box inside the optimal face nor a short
walk inside that face (up to ten primal steps that keep the optimum) made SC
end at such vertices. The second and third are engineering, and a revised
engine with the pricing and the updates of SoPlex would remove them. Until
then the hybrid uses each engine where it is better; on systems of equations,
whose best relaxation is \texttt{compo}, this is what makes the redesigned
contractor pay.

\paragraph{What did not pay.} Several ideas that use the certificate or the
tableau beyond the pass did not pay on the whole benchmark. Certificates of an
ancestor box remain valid (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}) but rarely prune what
the node does not prune anyway. Truncating each LP when its anytime bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mono}) is close to what the LP can still gain saves
pivots ($\times0.84$) but weakens the contraction (cells
$\times1.39$--$1.60$). Propagating with HC4 in the middle of a pass, cuts at
the LP optima, and a Lagrangian bound improved by monotonicity reduce the
cells or tighten bounds, but cost more than they save. Inheriting the basis of
the parent node cuts the pivots of the first LP of a node by a factor of three
or more, but the first LP is a small part of the node. Earlier versions of SC
kept the whole tableau explicitly and perturbed the zero reduced costs of the
highly dual-degenerate bound problems~\cite{koberstein2005dual}; with the
other components in place both made SC slower. Doing more work inside a node
did not change the search tree; what changed it was what is computed: the
merged relaxation, the stability filter of the ratio test, the Farkas ray of
the infeasible row, the tolerance.

\paragraph{Limits.} SC removes the external LP solver from the contractor, not
from the optimizer: the upper-bounding of IbexOpt still solves its LPs with
SoPlex. On problems with very narrow equalities the upper-bounding becomes the
bottleneck: on bearing, whose eight equalities are relaxed to
$|h|\le10^{-8}$, feasible points are found only in boxes of width $10^{-4}$ to
$10^{-3}$, and the search tree changes by two orders of magnitude between
seeds; we excluded it, with alkyl and alkylbis, from the means. Relaxations of
several hundred rows are still expensive for every engine: the systems of 400
to 1\,000 variables of the benchmark exceed the limits of 60\,s and 6\,GB with
all of them. And the gain of SC on the whole system solver is small, because
the pass is a small part of its nodes. One use of the certificate is left
open: when the warm-started simplex declares an infeasibility that the ray
does not certify, SC solves the LP again from the slack basis
(Section~\ref{sec:iter}), but the multipliers of the basis where it stopped,
moved along the ray as long as the bound improves, already give a certified
bound without that second LP.

\section{Conclusion}

A polytope-hull contractor solves $2n$ small, closely related LPs per node and
certifies each bound with the Neumaier--Shcherbina bound, which is valid for
any multipliers. A general-purpose LP solver cannot take advantage of this,
because the work it does to make its answer correct is internal to each call.
A simplex designed around the certificate omits that work: it verifies
nothing, does not refactorize before declaring an optimum, needs no phase~1 and
solves the $2n$ problems on one shared basis. Measured as a ladder inside the
same contractor, on the same LPs and with the same contraction, a pass of this
simplex costs 0.37--0.39 times that of SoPlex as Ibex uses it on relaxations of
up to 30 rows, and 22--26\% less than the best use of SoPlex, its simplex driven
directly. Inside the search, the redesigned contractor makes the optimizer 25\%
faster than with SoPlex as Ibex uses it, 7\% of it beyond the bare simplex. On
larger relaxations the pivots dominate and SoPlex needs fewer, mostly because
the vertices where it ends an LP are better starting points for the next
bound; a hybrid that chooses the engine by the size of the relaxation keeps
the best of both. How to choose, inside the optimal face of a degenerate
polytope, the vertex that serves the next bound is the open question.
